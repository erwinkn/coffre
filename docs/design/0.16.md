# 0.16: a smaller vault

The design for 0.16, written on 2026-11-13 before any of its code. It
replaces the vault, the contract between the app and the vault, and the
database schema. It is built on the 0.14 line, and a deployment upgrades
straight from 0.14: no deployment ever ran 0.15, whose design this replaces.

0.15 tried to refuse, on every use, anything a database writer had tampered
with. That cost a 1,164-line stored function with 31 arguments, a seal on 13
tables checked against the log on each use, and three generations of audit
tables. 0.16 promises less, on purpose, and gets most of it for a fraction of
the code.

The decisions are Erwin's, recorded as D215 to D237 in the Coffre Initiative.
The [list](#decisions) is at the end.

## In short

- **25 tables**, against 21 in 0.14.5 and 39 on the abandoned 0.15 line.
  0.16.0 ships 23 of them: `passkeys` and `proxy_routes` come with their
  features, in later releases (D227). They are `packages/db/src/schema.ts`.
- **Two request shapes**, a use and a change. Every API operation is one or
  the other.
- **One background job**, the signer, which runs outside the database.
- **No stored function, and no lock on a use.** SQL is plain selects, inserts
  and updates. Each decision is one TypeScript function.
- **A use costs 0 to 2 database calls and a change 2**, however large the log.

## What it promises

The adversary can write any row of any table. They hold neither the vault's
keys nor the signer's storage.

**Never honoured.** A forged or edited access row, since its MAC fails. A
value's row moved under another key, copied over another version of its own
key, or relabelled as plain or as not sensitive, since each version's
encryption names its secret, its version number and its nature (see
[Value encryption](#data-structures)).

**Detected within about a minute.** A genuine old row put back, a row
deleted. An entry not yet signed that is edited, deleted or replayed. A log
cut short or replaced at its end. The signer alerts and coffre keeps serving
(D216).

Until someone acts on the alert, a restored row works. Example: Ada revokes
Bo's grant, and a database writer puts the grant row back with its genuine
MAC. Bo's next use succeeds. Within a minute the signer sees a row its own
record says is gone, and alerts. An ordinary revocation, with nobody writing
to the database behind coffre's back, takes effect within the vault's short
memory, 2 seconds by default.

**Not caught within the minute.**

- A signed entry edited or deleted, once another has been signed after it,
  which on a busy instance is within a second. The signer re-checks a fixed
  slice of signed entries each minute, a page of the log checks each entry
  it shows, and `coffre verify --full` walks all of them (D222).
- An entry whose wake-up never left the vault, because the vault died between
  its commit and the call, and which the writer deletes within the second
  before signing. That one change can be undone silently.
- A row taken away for one decision and put back after it, which no check
  finds. A change's entry
  names every row its decision read, with its MAC, and the signer holds each
  to its mirror. A row the read looked for and did not find has no name to
  give. Example: the writer deletes the instance's policy row, a change that
  the policy would have refused goes through on the defaults, and the writer
  puts the row back. So the rule for every operation: a decision takes
  nothing from a missing row. With fewer rows it refuses, or allows less.
  The policy row is always there, and an operation that does not find it
  refuses. One decision does take something from rows that are not there:
  a sign-in takes an account with no identity as new, tries the next email
  when one names no member, and binds an account to a member who has none.
  A sign-in whose rows are taken away for it is a case this item accepts
  (see "Signing in"). So does a role's deletion, which takes a role no grant
  names as one to drop, and its definition again, which takes the slug as
  free (D240). The writer hides Bo's grant of `release` while an Owner
  deletes the role, and keeps it hidden until an Owner defines `release`
  again; put back then, it gives Bo the new role. Put back any earlier, it
  refuses the definition (`role_granted`), and gone at a minute's check, it
  alerts. It is not closed: that takes a sealed role incarnation on every grant, checked
  at every read of a grant, for a writer who must time two hides around an
  Owner deleting and re-creating one slug. So does a service's role on a
  whole project, and its tokens, which take the giver holding the role in
  each environment the read finds protected, as 0.14.7 weighed it (D241):
  Dee, a Developer of `shop` who only uses its protected `safe`, gives
  `token:wide` Developer on all of `shop` while the writer hides `safe`'s
  row, and `wide` reads `safe` once it is back. Sealing the list of
  protected environments would close it. An archive refused while a
  reference reads the key is a courtesy to that reference's readers, not access: a writer who deletes the
  reference's row gains nothing they could not do by deleting it alone.
  A service's grant taken away lets a person who did not manage it make
  its token, which holds only the scope sealed on its row, each grant one
  they hold all of: `ci` holds dev and prod, Bo is a Developer of dev, the
  writer deletes the prod grant while Bo makes a token and puts it back,
  and the token reads dev and is refused on prod, under `ci`'s name in a
  kept `token.create` entry with Bo as its actor. A personal token made
  while a protected environment's row is taken away is a token where the
  check would have refused, and reaches nothing more: its grant keeps
  what its person holds in the protected column on the whole project, so
  the environment back, it uses where they use.

**Three more, accepted.** Found by the expert reviews of the signer, in
what an entry says, not in how the signer checks it, and of the upgrade.

- *A row put back, used and withdrawn between two minute's checks* (D234). A
  use's entry names no row. The writer puts Bo's revoked grant back, Bo
  reads, the writer deletes the grant again 50 milliseconds later. The use
  is in the log under Bo's name and nothing alerts. The example above holds
  only while the row is still there at the next check. A change is held
  tighter: its entry names the rows it was decided on, so the same trick
  with a change alerts when it is signed.
- *An entry of a change that failed* (D235). A row and its entry are sealed
  before the database takes them. A trigger can copy both out and fail the
  statement; the vault answers 503 and tells the signer nothing. Within the
  minute the writer can insert that entry and row as genuine, if the row has
  not changed since. What the writer gains is small: a change an authorized
  caller asked for, and was told failed, happens up to a minute later. A 503
  on a change already means "it may have happened", since an answer can be
  lost after the commit.
- *A row planted on 0.14 and never used* (D237). The upgrade's adoption
  seals the rows 0.14's app signed as they stand, a session planted for a
  real Owner included ([What it trusts](#what-it-trusts)).

So the rule, the same for every operation: a database writer never gets
coffre to honour what the vault did not seal, at any time. What the vault
did seal they can put back, an old row of any age or an entry less than a
minute old, and coffre honours it until someone acts. What follows is an
alert within the minute, never a refusal, and in the cases listed above no
alert at all.

**Four smaller relaxations** (D217).

1. A use already running when its credential is revoked may finish.
2. Refusals give the same answer whether or not the key exists. They no longer
   take the same time. An invalid credential is still one uniform 401, before
   any lookup.
3. The bulk cap, 1,000 distinct keys per principal per 15 minutes, sliding and
   configurable, may be overshot by the requests in flight when it is reached.
4. A use is no longer one database call. The first use of a key in a minute is
   two, and its repeats are one or none.

## Four rules

1. **The vault writes every row, and puts a MAC on each one that turns into
   access.** The app's database login only reads.
2. **No request reads the log.** Requests only append to it.
3. **The signer, outside the database, holds what the database is held to:**
   the log's signed head, and its own copy of every access row's MAC.
4. **Every decision is one TypeScript function.** The same function decides a
   use and a change, and nothing in SQL repeats its rules.

## Tables

A starred table carries a MAC on each row, and the signer keeps a copy of it.

| Group | Tables |
|---|---|
| Secrets (5) | `projects`*, `environments`*, `secrets`*, `secret_versions`, `dismissed_keys`* |
| Access (5) | `principals`*, `grants`*, `roles`*, `policy`* (one row), `proxy_routes`* |
| Sign-in (4) | `identities`*, `passkeys`*, `bindings`* (CI trust), `credentials`* (sessions, tokens, MCP connections) |
| One-shot (5) | `device_logins`*, `enrollment_codes`*, `spent_id_tokens`, `oauth_clients`*, `mcp_approvals` |
| Log (6) | `log_pending`, `log`, `log_index`, `log_tiles`, `log_head` (one row), `exposures` |

- **Forks, personal configs and references are three columns**, not tables:
  `environments.parent_id`, `environments.owner` and `secrets.source_id`.
  TypeScript resolves them.
- **A restriction lives in the row it restricts.** "Protected" is a field of
  the environment's row, a freeze a field of the row it stops, and a role
  with no row is no role. So deleting a row can only remove access, never add
  it.
- **A grant is of its member's generation,** the one it was made in, as a
  credential is, and counts only while the member is in it.
- **Three of the one-shot tables are starred**, because a row of theirs turns
  into access: an approved device login becomes a session, an enrollment code
  a workload's session, and a registered client's redirects are where a
  person's approval is sent.
- **Versions carry no MAC.** Their encryption already binds them.
- **A secret's row says how its current value is kept and was made:** its
  nature (plain, secret or sensitive; none for a key with no value of its
  own) and, for a generated value, its recipe and its key pair. A change
  reads only rows under a MAC, so this is where a write finds what it writes
  over, and Regenerate the recipe it makes the next value by. The version's
  row keeps its own `sensitive` and `plaintext`, which its encryption names
  and a use opens it under, and the engine writes a value only as the nature
  its secret's row says. Example: `STRIPE_KEY` is sensitive; a writer who
  marks its row plain makes it a row the vault does not see, and one who
  relabels its version makes a value that does not open.
- **A dismissed key carries one too.** A fork that dismissed a key does not
  inherit it, so the row decides what a use of the fork is given.
- **A project's row carries one for its slug.** A request names a project by
  slug, and the vault resolves it. With no MAC, a database writer could swap
  the slugs of `market` and `billing`, and a deploy that holds both would be
  handed billing's values under market's name.
- **A secret says how many versions it has had, and which one is its value:**
  the newest, or none. A reference has no value of its own, and neither does
  a secret whose reference ended, until someone writes one. Example: a key
  with versions 1 and 2 is made a reference, and the reference is later
  broken. It has no value, its version 2 is history, and the next write is
  version 3.
- **A version says when its value was first written to its key**
  (`first_written_at`): its own date, or for a restore its source's, so a
  restore of a restore keeps the first. The offboarding report holds each
  person's last sight of a key (`exposures`) to it: the key is to rotate
  for them when they saw it on or after its current value's. Example: Bo
  last saw `STRIPE_KEY` on day 11; version 4 restores version 1, written on
  day 1, so Bo knows the current value and the report says rotate, where
  version 4's own date, day 12, would say he never saw it. A copy for the
  pages, as `plaintext`: under no MAC, and no decision reads it. Someone
  who edits it hides a key from the report, or adds one, as someone who
  edits `exposures` can.
- **`exposures` is the one summary kept beside its source.** It holds one row
  per principal and key: who has seen what, and when last. Requests may not
  read the log, and the cap, "last used" and the offboarding report all ask
  that question. It is bounded by principals times keys. It has no foreign
  key: a use writes it with no lock, and a foreign key's check would lock
  the principal's and the secret's rows, which a change may be writing.
- **0.14's audit history** becomes `audit_archive` at the
  [upgrade](#upgrade-from-014): the same rows, without their indexes but
  one, never written again, exportable from the CLI. The new log's first
  entry records the old chain's last hash. Pages show the new log only.

## Data structures

**Row MAC.** An HMAC-SHA256 over the table's name, the row's id and its
content, under a key derived from the vault's. The vault checks it on every
read and refuses a row that fails. A MAC starts with four bytes that name its
key, so the vault knows which key to check and a
[rotation](#rotating-the-vaults-key) knows which rows are left: 36 bytes in
all.

- **It leaves out what only says when a row was last used:** a credential's
  and a binding's last use. A credential's moves on with no change, at a
  use, and a binding's with the change that uses it, beside its row and
  not in it. So a database writer can falsify them and nothing else. A
  change that writes such a row leaves them as they are: it would put back
  the time it read.
- **It leaves out a column that holds null.** So a nullable column added in
  a later release leaves every MAC made before it good.
- **Every time is a `timestamptz(3)`,** milliseconds, which the vault
  supplies. So a row reads back exactly as the vault wrote it, and no
  database default decides a value a MAC covers.

**Value encryption.** Each version has its own data key, which encrypts the
value, and the vault's key wraps the data key. The wrap names the secret, a
personal config's owner, whether the value is sensitive and, new in 0.16,
the version's number and whether the value is plain (`envelope_version` 2,
`@coffre/core/envelope`).

- **Why the number.** Without it, a database writer could copy the row of a
  key's version 1, a credential since rotated away, over its version 3, and
  every reader would be handed the old one. Putting back the secret's own
  row, so that it names version 1 as current again, is another matter: that
  row is genuine, and the signer's minute check catches it, like any row
  put back.
- **Why plain.** A plain value is read by whoever lists its environment, and
  by a workload whose binding does not list it. Were plain only a column, a
  writer could mark a secret plain and have it handed to them.
- **0.16 opens format 2 only.** A value 0.14 wrote is `envelope_version` 1,
  and its wrap names neither. The vault's [adoption](#the-steps) of a 0.14
  database wraps every key again, and after it no format-1 row opens.
  Otherwise a 0.14 row kept aside could be written over any later version
  of its secret, labelled 1, and open. A version 0.14 erased has no key to
  wrap and keeps its label: it opens as nothing, in either format.
- **A plain value's `plaintext` column is a copy for the pages.** It is
  under no MAC, so a use never answers from it: it opens the ciphertext like
  any other value's, as plain. Whatever hands a plain value to a process (an
  export, `coffre run`, an env file) is a use, not a page.
- **What a version's row holds that nothing binds:** who wrote it and when,
  the `plaintext` copy, and its id. Each only displays, but the id, which a
  regenerate names as the version it replaces: a writer who moves it can
  make a stale regenerate go through, or a fresh one stale, and nothing
  more, as in 0.14.7. How a value was generated
  decides how Regenerate makes the next one, so it is on the secret's row,
  under its MAC: a writer who set `bytes:32:hex` to `bytes:1:hex` there
  would make a row no decision sees.

**The log.** Requests insert into `log_pending`, each entry with its own MAC.
About a second later the signer moves the waiting entries into `log` and
numbers them. It is the only writer of `log`, so numbering takes no lock.

`log` has four columns: an entry's place in the log's order, which tree it is
under, its place in that tree, and its body, the canonical JSON its hash
covers. It has no MAC, since the tree vouches for it once signed, and no time
of its own: an entry's time is in its body, and the signer reads it there
before it prunes, so a time edited in a column could not make it prune early.

The entries are one table and one order, under two Merkle trees (RFC 9162):
one over the entries kept for good, the changes, and one over those that
[age out](#how-long-the-log-is-kept), the uses and what else that section
names (D229). A tree prunes cleanly
only from its start, hence two. An entry has its place in the log's order and
its place in its tree.

- `log_tiles` holds each tree's hashes, stored 256 to a row. Proving one
  entry reads at most 8 rows, whatever the log's size.
- `log_index` holds one row per object an entry mentions: project,
  environment, key, principal, credential, as `secret:<id>` and the like.
  Every filtered audit page reads that one index, its primary key.
- `log_head` holds both trees' sizes and roots under one MAC, written in one
  statement, so the two never disagree about when. It is a copy for the
  pages. The signer's own copy is the one that counts, and its minute check
  holds this row to it.

**The signer's mirror.** The signer keeps, in its own storage, the MAC of
every starred row. Each change entry records the MAC it replaced and the MAC
it wrote, and the signer takes an entry only if the MAC it replaced is the
one the mirror holds. Once a minute it reads the rows and compares them with
the mirror.

A running hash over the rows was the first design, and the model broke it:

1. Ada grants Bo access, and the writer deletes the grant row.
2. Ada sees Bo missing and grants again. That entry says "was: nothing",
   which is false history. A sum cannot tell.
3. Ada later revokes Bo, and the writer puts the row back.
4. Two grants less one revoke leave one grant, the rows show one grant, and
   the check passes.

With the mirror, step 2 alerts at signing, because the mirror still holds the
first grant's MAC. The mirror costs one MAC per access row in the signer's
storage, a few megabytes at 100,000 rows.

**The wake-up** (D223). After a commit, the vault calls the signer with the
hash of the entry it wrote. The call does not hold the reply. It wakes the
signer, which otherwise would poll an idle database, and it tells the signer
which entries to expect, so one deleted before signing is missed. Without it,
a trigger in the database could undo every revocation of Bo and delete its
entry, and nothing would ever differ from the last signed state.

**The vault's short memory.** An instance keeps the rows a use read, and
the values it opened from them, for a configurable number of seconds, 2 by
default, 0 for never (D218). It dates them when it read the rows, not when
it answered, and decides on them again at each use, so a credential that
expires within those seconds stops at once. It also remembers which
credential it logged reading which key in the last 60 seconds.
0.14.5 already has a `cacheSeconds`, which Equisafe sets to 330.

**Through an outage of the database (D238).** A deployment may set
`outageSeconds` (0 by default, an hour at most). While the database
answers, it changes nothing a caller sees: the memory above is the only
one, for every caller. When a use's read fails because the database could
not be reached, a proxied use (the value is never handed back) is answered
from the rows this instance last answered the same ask from as a proxied
use, for `outageSeconds` after it read them, whatever the memory's length. An ask is a credential, a
place and its keys, so the use is given the versions it was given and
logged for before, and nothing else: no newer version, no new key, nothing
the bulk cap has refused this ask since. The cap itself is not counted
again in an outage: its window may have slid past what the ask was given. No reveal, `coffre run`, page or change is answered
this way.

- The decision is made again as of now from those rows, so a credential
  that expires during the outage stops.
- The memory keeps the newest rows read for an ask. A read that answers
  takes the place of the older ones whatever becomes of its use. So a use
  whose read answered and whose write did not fails as the database did.
  A refusal forgets the ask's rows, and the vault remembers when it
  refused, for `outageSeconds`, the last thousand asks refused: rows read
  before that are not kept again, so a slower use that read
  before a revocation does not put them back for an outage to be answered
  from. Rows are dated when their read began: two reads a revocation falls
  between, begun in the other order, escape this, as a change the rows do
  not hold does. One of the bulk cap's refusals leaves the rows for the
  memory's length, and for no outage. A refusal of a reveal or a peek
  forgets the same ask's proxied rows too.
- What an outage cannot see is a change those rows do not hold: one made
  since they were read, or made through another instance that still
  reaches the database. Either waits `outageSeconds` at most. For a
  credential in steady use the first is the memory's length before the
  outage; for one last used four minutes before it, four minutes. 0.14's
  cache made every revocation wait `cacheSeconds` for proxied requests,
  outage or not.
- An outage is the database out of reach: no connection, one lost, none
  left in the pool. A statement that gave up behind a lock, or ran past its
  bound, is not, and neither is a key service that does not answer: that
  use fails, as before. Whoever can keep the database out of reach, by
  filling its pool for one, keeps a credential revoked meanwhile in use for
  `outageSeconds` at most.
- The rows are kept, with the values opened from them, for `outageSeconds`
  from their read, where everything else the vault remembers goes within
  the memory's length: an hour at most, as 0.14's cache.
- Nothing can be logged meanwhile. A use that was due an entry is counted,
  by credential, and that credential's next logged proxied use says since
  when and how many (`unlogged` in its detail). The count is each use,
  where the log would say one a minute. It may be said twice (uses side by
  side, or a write that landed and whose answer was lost), and is lost with
  its instance, past a thousand credentials, or when the credential makes
  no proxied use again.
- A failure is remembered for two seconds, whatever the memory's length:
  proxied uses in them are answered at once, and the next one asks the
  database again. So one lost connection, with the database there, has
  proxied uses answered from their older rows for two seconds. The first
  uses of an outage wait for their read to fail, which is the connection's
  to bound (Hyperdrive: 15 seconds for a connection; a statement that
  hangs has no bound of the vault's own yet).
- At most a thousand asks are kept, as for the memory: past that, the
  oldest go, and are not answered in an outage.

## Operations

**The contract** between the app and the vault is five calls
(`@coffre/core/vault`), where 0.14 had 17. Every argument and answer is plain
JSON, so the same call crosses a Workers service binding, a process boundary
or nothing at all.

| Call | What it is | Database calls |
|---|---|---|
| `use` | Open values, as below | 0 to 2, and one more per credential every five minutes |
| `peek` | What a use would give, each key's secret, version and nature or why not, opening and writing nothing; for `coffre run`'s generating, each key's version id and recipe, whether it is the caller's own, and whether the read found their personal config | 0 or 1 |
| `caller` | Who a request's credential is and what it holds, for the app to scope a page's queries with | 1 |
| `change` | One named operation with its input, `{ op: 'grant.set', … }` | 2 |
| `weigh` | What that change would answer, with nothing written and no key service asked: a dry run (`?dryRun=1`) | 1, and one more for a refusal it logs |

A `use` or a `peek` answers from the vault's memory of its rows for
`memorySeconds`; a caller that has just changed what it reads asks
`fresh`, which reads the rows now and remembers them in place of the old
ones. Only `coffre run`'s plan does, after the values it generated were
written; a proxied use, a reveal and a version poll never do. A key a
peek refuses is its name and why, and no version id or recipe.

Every call starts with what the request showed: the SHA-256 of its token,
the thumbprint of its proof's key if it has one, its id and its address. The
app never says who is asking. In 0.14 it found the credential in its own
tables and named a principal, which the vault took on its word.

Example: Ada runs `coffre get market/prod/DATABASE_URL`. The app hashes her
session's token and calls `use({ token, project: 'market', environment:
'prod', keys: ['DATABASE_URL'], purpose: 'reveal', … })`. The vault finds her
session by the hash, decides, opens the value and answers
`{ principal: 'user:ada@acme.example', values: [{ key: 'DATABASE_URL',
value: 'postgres://…', … }] }`. The app never held a key, a ciphertext or a
grant.

A refusal is an answer, `{ refused: 'no_access' }`, and writes nothing of
what was asked. A refused use writes nothing at all, and a refused change
one log entry that says so (below). Only a fault throws: a bug, or the
database down, which the app answers 503.

A change's operation has the name of its log entry's action, `grant.set`,
`secret.write`: one decision function each, beside the others of its area,
in `packages/vault/src/ops/<area>.ts`, with what it takes and answers in
`packages/core/src/changes/<area>.ts`.
One operation logs one of three actions: `member.put` admits a member,
brings one back or sets their role (`member.add`, `member.restore`,
`member.role`), whichever their row calls for, as 0.14.7's `admit` did,
since only the vault's read of that row knows which, and the app's invite,
role change, `coffre members add` and `coffre verify` ask it as one call.
A service account given its first grants (`access.grant` with `admit`,
0.14.7's `setAccess`) is admitted in the same change, all or none, by
whoever could set one up there: its one entry is `access.grant`, whose
detail names the admission (`admitted: 'member.add'` or `'member.restore'`).

What became of 0.14's calls:

| 0.14 | 0.16 |
|---|---|
| `unwrap` | `use`. The app names a place and keys, not stored versions, and is given values, not data keys: the vault reads the versions and opens them |
| `wrap`, `rewrap` | `change`: a value written, restored or made stricter. The app sends the value and the vault seals it, since only the vault writes |
| `reference`, `endReferences` | `change`: a reference is its secret's `source_id` |
| `access` | `caller` |
| `setAccess`, `admit`, `remove`, `setSettings`, `changePolicy` | `change` |
| `settings`, `policy` | No call. They are rows, which the app reads to show them. A decision that depends on them is the vault's, on rows whose MAC it checked |
| `checkpoint` | Gone: the signer signs the log, on its own clock |
| `verifyLog` | Gone: the audit page and `coffre verify` read the tiles. The vault vouches for the head to the app: `caller` with `head` answers the one under its MAC |
| `about`, `keyChecks` | `about`, with the Settings page and `coffre restore`'s check of a backup |

*Built so far:* `use`, `peek`, `caller`, `weigh`, and `change` with its first operations
(`packages/vault/src/vault.ts`, `ops/`). `about` comes with Settings.

**A use** is a reveal, `coffre run`, an export, a proxied request, a
signature, an MCP read (`packages/vault/src/use.ts`).

*What the app sends.* The SHA-256 of the token the caller presented; the
place, as a project's and an environment's slugs; whether it is the caller's
personal config of that environment (`@me`); the keys by name, 1,000 at
most, or none for every key; the purpose; and the request's id and address,
for the log. The purpose `use` means the value is used for the caller by the
credential proxy and never handed back, which takes the `secret.use`
permission. Any other purpose hands the value back and takes `secret.read`.
Two more, for the callers that need them: the upstream credential the values
are used for, by its name in the deployment's configuration (`stripe`), and
the thumbprint of the key that signed the request's proof (RFC 9449), once
the app has held the proof to the request.

*What the vault does.*

1. If it read these rows less than 2 seconds ago, it decides on them again
   as of now, with no read. The write of step 5 still happens if it is
   owed, say for another purpose.
2. Otherwise, one read: the credential by its hash, its principal, linked
   account and grants; the roles; for a credential a trust binding issued,
   the binding; for a workload's session, the sessions it was narrowed from
   and the instance's policy row; the project, the environment and its
   parents; the keys asked for, the secrets they refer to and where those
   live; the dismissed keys; each current version. A row whose MAC is not the
   vault's is left out, as if it were not there.
3. It decides, in one function of those rows and the time.
4. It opens each value it decided to give.
5. For the keys this credential was not logged reading in the last 60
   seconds, one statement: it moves `exposures` on, counts the principal's
   keys in the cap's window, and inserts the log entry. Past the cap, the
   statement writes nothing and the whole use is refused.
6. It answers, then remembers the rows, dated when it read them.

So a use costs 0 to 2 database calls. Once every five minutes per credential
it costs one more, a statement of its own that moves the credential's
last-used time on. That statement updates one row, skips it if a change
holds it, and never puts an earlier time back. Nothing a use writes is
written by a change or points at a row one writes, so a use and a change
never wait on each other. The read also selects the database's clock, which
keeps a pooler from answering it out of a cache (Hyperdrive's is off in
every deployment besides).

*What comes back.* Who asked (principal and credential), and one answer per
key: its value, with the secret that holds it, its version number and
whether it is sensitive, or why this caller is not given it. For a
workload's session, also its binding's policy and its own limits on methods
and paths, which only the app can hold the request to. A use is refused as a
whole only for who asks or where:

| Refused as a whole | When |
|---|---|
| `unauthenticated` | No such credential, or it is expired, revoked or frozen, or its member was removed since, or its linked account was unlinked, or the binding that issued it was removed. For a workload's session: no proof by its key, past its hard end, or a session above it ended. One answer for all |
| `pending`, `no_reads`, `source`, `frozen` | A workload's session that stands and is stopped: it waits for approval; the deployment lets no binding list values to hand back, and its binding does; the request comes from outside its binding's addresses; the instance, its service, its binding, it or a session above it is frozen. None ends the session: lifting the cause restores service with no new sign-in |
| `narrowed` | A narrowed session asks to be handed values, or uses them outside the places and upstream credentials it was narrowed to |
| `no_access` | The place does not exist, is archived or deleted, or the caller does not hold there what the purpose takes: nothing at all, or `secret.use` alone when asking to be handed values. One answer for all, so that a refusal does not say whether a place exists |
| `frozen` | The upstream credential named is frozen in this environment, for everyone. Told only to a caller who may use the place |
| `broken_chain` | A parent of the environment is gone, or it has more than three |
| `bulk_limit` | This read would take the principal past 1,000 distinct keys in 15 minutes |

| Refused for one key | When |
|---|---|
| `unknown_key` | No such key, or it is archived, or the fork dismissed it |
| `no_value` | The key has no value yet |
| `parent_unreadable` | The key is inherited from a parent the caller may not read |
| `sensitive` | A sensitive value, asked for by a person to be handed back |
| `not_owner_issued` | A sensitive value, asked for by a service account on a credential no Owner of the value's project issued |
| `use_only` | A workload's binding does not list the key |
| `unreadable` | A reference whose source is archived, deleted, empty or itself a reference; or a version that does not open as what its rows say it is |

Example: `coffre run` in `market/staging`, a fork of `market/prod`, by Bo,
who holds a grant on staging alone. He gets staging's own `DATABASE_URL`, and
`parent_unreadable` for `STRIPE_KEY`, which staging inherits from prod.

*How keys resolve.* The environment first, then its parents, nearest first:
a key is the nearest one's. A key the fork dismissed stops there. With `@me`,
the caller's personal config comes first, and someone with none reads the
environment itself. A personal config's keys are its owner's alone, while
they read or use the environment it is a config of. A reference is read by
whoever reads the environment it lives in, takes its value and its
sensitivity from its source, and is counted and logged under its source.

*What is logged.* One entry per use, naming the keys given that this
credential had not been logged reading, for this purpose and at this
version, in the last 60 seconds, wherever it asked. So a key read in
`billing/prod` and again within the minute through a reference in
`market/prod` has one entry, under the secret that holds the value. A
refusal writes nothing. What a credential was logged reading is remembered
by the instance that wrote its entry, once written, so uses of one key side
by side, or in another instance, each write an entry.

*Who asks* (`packages/vault/src/caller.ts`), by the credential's kind:

| Credential | Stands while | Reaches |
|---|---|---|
| Browser or CLI session | Its member is active in the generation it was issued in, and its linked account is not unlinked | All its member holds |
| Service or personal token | The same | Its member's holdings, within the token's scope |
| Handle | The same | The same: `viewer` where its person reads, so that `coffre run` reads its plan through it. The app keeps it off the API |
| Token a trust binding issued (a CI run's) | Also: the binding is not removed, is its service's, and was bound in the same generation | Its member's holdings, within the binding's scope, as it is now |
| Workload's session | Also: the request's proof is signed by the session's key, its hard end is not past, and every session it was narrowed from stands | Its member's holdings, within its scope or its binding's. It is handed only the keys its binding lists, where the value is held |

A removal moves its member's generation on, so what was issued or granted
before it reaches nothing after, whatever its own row says: a grant a
writer hid from the removal and put back is dead when the member is let
back in, as a session kept aside is.

Example: `api`, a workload bound to `market` with `reads` listing prod's
`DATABASE_URL`. Its session reveals `DATABASE_URL` in `market/prod`, gets
`use_only` for `STRIPE_KEY`, and has `STRIPE_KEY` used for it by the proxy.
In `market/staging`, which inherits prod's `STRIPE_KEY` and has its own
`DATABASE_URL`, it gets `use_only` for both: staging's is another secret,
which the binding does not list.

A deployment with `useOnly` takes no service token at all, and one that
configures no workloads takes no session.

*Not built yet.* A call from another Worker over a Cloudflare service
binding presents no token of its own, so the vault has no row to find it
by: it names a service and proves nothing (see "A workload signing in",
below). The vault does not answer it. An MCP connection's request comes
with an access token that has a row of its own ("An MCP connection",
below). The app keeps what only it can
do: refusing a token that is not shaped like one before any lookup, and
checking a workload's proof against the request.

A browser session whose provider's issuer is no longer the configured one
lasts until it expires or is revoked: see "Signing in", below.

*What changed from 0.14.* 0.14 told an expired grant from none, and a
missing place from a forbidden one, and refused a whole read when one key
of it could not be read. 0.16 gives one `no_access`, and answers key by key.
0.14 would have read a workload's freezes at every request, so that a
freeze stopped it at once (nothing in 0.14.7 writes one). 0.16 reads them with the caller's other rows, so a freeze, like any
revocation, takes up to the vault's memory, 2 seconds (D218).

A batch of up to 1,000 keys is the same two statements.

**Who a request is** (`caller`, in `packages/vault/src/caller.ts`). A page
reads the app's own tables, which no use or change decides: the list of
projects, an environment's key names. For those the app asks the vault who
the request's credential is and all it holds, once a request, and scopes its
queries with the answer.

*What the app sends.* What the request showed, and nothing else.

*The read.* One statement, by the token's hash: what a change reads of its
caller (`callerParts`), over every project instead of one. That is the
credential, its principal and linked account, the binding that issued it,
all its grants and the roles they name, the member's role in the instance,
each environment a grant is on, and every protected environment of the
instance, since a role holds less in one. `callerOf` decides on those rows
as it does for a use and a change. A handle is refused, as for a change: it
is the proxy's.

*The answer.* `{ principal, credential: { id, kind, expiresAt }, holds }`.
`holds` is what `allows` in `@coffre/core/access` decides on: the instance
role and its scope, the grants, a token's own scope, the roles' definitions
and the protected environments. The app asks `allows(holds, 'secret.read',
place)` with the function the vault uses, counting its own row's
`protected` flag too, as the vault does for a change (`may`): an
environment whose row someone edited is missing from the vault's list, and
is protected still. Anything else is
`{ refused: 'unauthenticated' }`: one answer for a token that is unknown,
expired, revoked, or a removed member's. A workload's session that is
stopped is told why, as at a use, and a narrowed one is refused: it reaches
no page.

*What the answer is for.* It scopes what a page lists: names, never values.
Nothing is opened or changed on it. A use and a change read the caller
again and decide for themselves, so an app that got the answer wrong, or
kept it too long, shows a list it should not and opens nothing.

*Memory.* The rows are remembered for the vault's memory, 2 seconds, under
the token's hash, and decided on again at each call, so a session that
expires within those seconds stops at once. A refusal is not remembered.
The call writes nothing but the credential's last use, every five minutes
at most, as a use does.

*The log's head, when asked.* The audit page sends `head: true`, and the
same statement reads `log_head` beside the caller's rows. The vault holds
the row to its MAC (the entries' key, over both trees' sizes and roots,
`aging_from` and the signing time) and answers with it, or with `head: null`
when no head stands under its keys. The app holds no key, and the row, the
tiles and the entries are all a database writer's to edit, so a proof
against a head the app read itself would prove nothing. Against this one it
does: the page proves each entry it shows from the tiles it reads. A head is
never answered from memory, since it moves at each signing; the rows read
with it are remembered as any call's.

What the page may say of an entry follows from one fact: the signer writes
an entry into `log` and the head that covers it in the same statement. So an
entry of `log` past the head's sizes is one of three things: signed between
the page's two reads (the vault's, then the app's), there with an older head
a writer put back, or planted. The page cannot tell which, and says so:
*not proven*, never *waiting to be signed* (what waits is in `log_pending`,
which no page lists). A reload settles the first case. Example: the head
says 769 uses and the page lists uses 760 to 772. Uses 760 to 768 prove
against `agingRoot`. Uses 769 to 772 show as not proven; on a reload the head
says 773 and they prove, or they stay unproven, which is a finding for
whoever is looking.

This is the limit [the head's MAC](#how-long-the-log-is-kept) already
has: it says the vault signed this log, not that it signed none since. So
the signer holds the row itself to the head it signed last. It keeps that
head in its storage, written with the move that signs it, and its minute
check reads `log_head` in the statement that reads the rows and the log's
ends, and compares every column. An older head put back, one edited (its MAC
with it or not) or none is a finding within the minute, named as a row is
(`row`, of `["log_head"]`), once, and the next signing writes the right head
over it. Until then the page shows what the older head covers as proven and
the rest as not; a head edited under its MAC is answered to no caller
(`head: null`).

Example: the signer signs entry 41, then 42. A writer puts back the head
that covered 41, whole, MAC included. `caller` answers it, since the MAC is
the vault's, and the audit page shows entry 42 as not proven. Within a
minute the signer's check reads a head that is not the one it kept for 42,
and says so.

Example: Ada opens the Projects page. Its render makes several API calls in
process. The first asks `caller({ token, … })`, one statement, and the
others are given the same answer. The app then lists the projects `holds`
reaches: those of her grants, or every project for an Owner of the
instance.

*What grows.* The read returns the caller's grants and the instance's
protected environments: a few rows for most people, one per protected
environment on an instance that protects every `prod`.

**A change** is anything that writes: secrets, places, members, grants, roles,
the policy, tokens, bindings, a sign-in.

1. One read: the caller's rows and the rows to change.
2. Check the MACs, run the operation's `check` if it names one, decide
   with the same function, build the new rows and the log entry. A row whose MAC is not the vault's is no row to the decision,
   which is told how many each part of its read found (`unsealed`): one
   that allows more for a row that is not there refuses on it.
3. One write: take the change lock, update each row only if its MAC is still
   the one read, insert the log entry. If a row moved, start again.
4. Reply at commit (D221), then wake the signer.

The lock is one lock that only changes take, for that one statement. The
write compares every row the decision read, including rows it only counted.
The entry names each of those rows with its MAC: the rows written, with the
MAC replaced and the MAC written, and the rows only read, with the MAC they
carried. Example: Bo was an Owner and is now a Member. The writer puts Bo's
old Owner row back, Bo promotes Eve, and the writer withdraws the row, all
within a second. The entry says "Eve: Member to Owner, decided on Bo's row
with MAC `3fa8…`", the signer's mirror holds another MAC for Bo, and it
reports false history at its next run, with no row left to compare.

An entry is dated once the rows are read, as its write is sent, and the
write is bounded: 2 seconds for the change lock, then 5 seconds for the
write itself, in which no lock on a row is waited for longer than 2. Past
either the change answers 503, having written nothing. A use's entry is
dated the same way, after its values are opened, however long a key service
took; it takes no change lock, so its write has the 5 seconds alone. A
change that finds its rows changed starts over, up to 5 times, reading
again and dated anew each time.

So an entry commits within 7 seconds of its date, once its transaction has
a connection. When it has to wait for one, a use answered from memory, or a
connection lost since the read, that wait comes first, after the date. On
Workers, Hyperdrive gives a connection within 15 seconds or fails the call: 22
seconds in all, inside the minute the signer allows, so a database that is
slow never has it refuse an entry as too old. *Not built yet:* on Node, the
pool's queue has no bound. Behind a database slow enough, a write could
wait a minute for its connection, commit, and be reported as too old, a
false alert. Node bounds that wait, or dates the entry once the connection
is in hand, before it ships.

Time changes no row, so a decision that goes by the time left says how long
it holds. A session is renewed only while it has more than 5 seconds left,
and a renewal decided with 6 left, whose write then waits 7 for a connection
and the change lock, would bring back a session that had ended. Such a
decision names the instant it holds until, on the vault's clock, and the
write carries the span from the decision to it: its statement, once it has
the lock, checks that the database's clock has moved less than that since
the read. So the database's clock measures the wait at both ends, and need
not agree with the vault's. A write that comes late writes nothing and
starts the change over, as behind a row that changed: decided again, at a
later time, the renewal is refused `expired`. An operation leaves the 5
seconds a write's statement may run between that instant and what it must
land ahead of, so the renewal is written before the session ends, as
0.14's was, which compared inside its UPDATE.

The caller's own time is not one of those. A change is decided at one
instant, once its rows are read, by the credential and the grants that stand
then; its entry is dated as its write is sent, at that instant or later (a
value sealed through a key service comes between), and the write lands within
the 7 seconds above, or not at all. A credential or a grant that expires in
between does not stop it, and the entry may then be dated past that expiry;
one revoked, frozen or deleted in between, or its member removed, is a row
that changed, read again under the lock with the change's own
(`callerParts`, `packages/vault/src/caller.ts`), so the change starts over
and is decided again.

It compares by reading again. An operation names the rows it depends on as a
few conditions ("the principal with this id", "every active Owner"). The
write runs the same conditions under the lock and requires the same MACs, no
more and no fewer. So a row that came since the read stops the write as
surely as one that changed or went: two people admitting Bo at once both read
"nobody is named Bo", and the second write finds one.

The lock is taken by a statement of its own, in one transaction with the
write. A statement sees the database as of when it starts, so a write that
took the lock itself would compare against rows from before it waited, and
the three Owners below would all pass. The driver sends BEGIN, both
statements and COMMIT together, so the write is one round trip, and a change
two, which a test counts through a proxy.

An operation is two things: the conditions it reads by, and its decision, a
function from those rows to the rows to write and the log entry. At times a
third, a `check` run on the rows read, before the decision (see "A workload
signing in"). It has no
database in hand. Reading, comparing, sealing each row, logging the MAC each
write replaced and starting again are written once, in
`packages/vault/src/change.ts`. A change that loses five times in a row
answers 503, and so does one that waits two seconds for the lock or for a
row another session holds. Its read is bounded the same way, in a
transaction of its own sent in one round trip: a lock someone holds on a
table it reads makes it busy, and never hangs it, nor the signer's minute.

**Who asks a change** is read with its other rows, by the token's hash
(`callerParts` in `packages/vault/src/caller.ts`): the credential, its
principal and linked account, the binding that issued it, and of its grants
and roles those that bear on the project the change is in. Not all of them:
the entry names every row read, and an Owner's hundred grants elsewhere
decide nothing here. `callerOf` then decides on those rows exactly as it
does for a use, but for a handle, which is the proxy's and changes nothing.
The caller's read also takes the environments that weigh on what they
hold in that project: those it protects, and those a grant of theirs is
on. So no operation has to remember them, a decision about the whole
project (a personal token's role there, say) sees every protected
environment, and one protected or unprotected meanwhile starts the change
over. An archived environment protects as before, a deleted one nothing.

Example: Bo, a Developer of `market`, writes `DATABASE_URL` in
`market/prod`. The read finds his session, his principal, his grant on
`market`, the Developer role, the member role of the instance, the
project, the environment, the key's row, and each other environment
`market` protects. The entry names them all with their MACs, and replaces
one.

**A change weighed** (`weigh`, `packages/vault/src/change.ts`) is the same
operation with the same input, run up to where a change seals its values:
the read, the check, the decision, and what it writes held to what it read.
Then nothing is sealed and nothing is sent. It answers what the change
would, or refuses as it would, in one round trip. So a dry run is not a
second copy of a rule in the app. Example, once the routes ask it:
`coffre trust` previews a binding with `token.bind` weighed, shows the
places it would reach, then makes it with the same input.

What follows from writing nothing:

- No key service is asked anything. Each call to one is paired with a log
  entry (`docs/keys.md`), and a weighing writes none: a restore previewed
  would otherwise leave a Decrypt in CloudTrail that nothing explains.
- What only the write tells, a weighing answers as if it were not there: a
  version the write opens (one restored, or one whose label it goes by)
  that does not open (`unreadable`), which only a row edited behind the
  vault gives; and a fault in the values a decision writes, which is a bug
  in an operation and not a caller's doing.
- The answer is as of its read: the change made after reads and decides
  again, on rows that may have moved.
- No proof is spent and no binding's last use moves. None of the changes a
  caller asks does either today; this holds the engine, whatever is weighed.

Only what a caller asks is weighed. A sign-in, a device code redeemed, an
exchange or a workload's sign-in rests on a proof, which a weighing would
try without spending: the vault refuses to weigh those, as a fault.

A weighing refused is logged as the change refused is, with the same entry
and under the same once a minute (D236): the log does not tell a preview
from an attempt, and both say the caller asked for what they may not do. A
weighing that would be made leaves nothing. It hands back nothing a change
does not, and no change's answer carries a value.

**A write's dry run** (`PATCH /api/secrets/market/prod?dryRun=1`,
`packages/server/src/api/routes/secrets.ts`) answers 0.14's four outcomes
per key, `added`, `changed`, `unchanged` or `archived`, and never a value,
so that `coffre import` writes only what differs. `secret.write` answers
each key's outcome (`outcomes`, `KeyOutcome` in
`packages/core/src/changes/secrets.ts`), weighed or made, from what its
decision already knows: a key made, a key archived, a reference to the
source it reads already, a key filed where it is. Whether a value typed
over the key's own is the same takes opening that version, which a
decision never does, so that key's outcome is `compare`, with the secret
and version it is written over: only a value of the key's own, live, not
made by a recipe or in a pair, of the nature it would be written as, with
nothing else asked of it. The app then asks one use for those keys,
purpose `compare`, which opens them as a reveal would, for whoever reads
them, logs a `secret.read` and counts toward the bulk limit, as 0.14's dry
run did. A key is `unchanged` only if the use handed back that very
secret and version, and the value is the one in the patch, compared in
constant time; anything else is `changed`: a key the use refuses (a
sensitive value to a person, a use-only one, the caller who does not read
there), a use refused whole, a version written between the two calls, a
value read through a reference or from a parent, which the vault never
names. Example: Bo imports `DATABASE_URL=postgres://märkt` over that same
value at version 1: `weigh` names `{ compare: { secret, version: 1 } }`,
the use hands back version 1 with that value, and the answer is
`unchanged`; `STRIPE_KEY`, sensitive, is `changed`, and its value is never
opened. The write itself never compares: asked with the same value, it
writes the next version. Cost: one `weigh`, and one `use` only if a key is
`compare` (one per 1,000 such keys, a use's most), and no statement of
the app's.

Two differences from 0.14.7, both accepted. 0.14 refused the whole dry run
to a caller without `secret.read`; here a writer who does not read gets
one, every value they cannot read `changed`, which tells them nothing the
write's own answer does not (whether the key is there, archived or not).
And 0.14 compared a plain value with its plaintext column, unlogged; here
it is opened through the use like any value, logged and counted, since
that column is a copy for the pages under no MAC, which no decision reads.

**A refused change is logged** (D236), once a minute for whoever asked and
what they asked. Its entry is among those that age out, with its
operation's action and, in its detail, why it was refused (`refused`, which
is what tells it from the entry of a change that was made) and the place
as the caller named it. Example: Bo, a Viewer of `market`, tries to write
`DATABASE_URL` in `market/prod` at 09:14. He is answered `no_access`, and
the log has "09:14, Bo, `secret.write`, `market/prod`, refused:
`no_access`", found from Bo's page and, on the Audit page, from
`market/prod`. A loop that tries again all minute adds nothing.

- Every refusal to a caller who stands, whatever the reason: `no_access`,
  and also `archived` or a workload's `frozen`.
- It names the first refusal of the minute. Bo refused in `billing/prod`
  and then in 59 other places within the minute has one entry, for
  `billing/prod`.
- The log takes no free text from a caller: a place's name is logged only
  if it could be a slug, and as nothing otherwise.
- It costs a refused change one statement more, without the change lock,
  and never its answer: if the entry cannot be written, the refusal stands
  and the vault's own log says so. Refusals side by side write one entry
  between them.
- Not logged: a caller who does not stand, with an unknown, expired or
  revoked token, who is told `unauthenticated` and nothing else, as in
  0.14; and a refused use.
- The minute is each instance's own memory, so two instances may each log
  one.

**A value** is the one thing a change writes that carries no MAC, since a
version's encryption names what it is. The decision says what the version
holds, "version 3 of this key, this text, sensitive", and the engine seals
it after the decision and before the write: sealing draws a key and may
call a key service, which no decision does. The version's row goes in the
same statement as its secret's, so the two are there together or neither
is: the engine takes a value only as the current version of a secret the
same change writes, in an environment of its project that it read or
makes, of the nature that secret's row says, and seals it under what those
two rows say, a personal config's owner included. A change that
starts over seals again, under the number it then reads. A value copied
from a stored version, a restore, is opened by the engine and sealed again
the same way, never handed to the app nor to the decision: the change
reads the version's row, the one table without a MAC a change reads, and
the engine opens it as that row labels it, in the secret and environment
its row names, before anything is written. A version relabelled or erased
does not open, and a plain one whose copy in its row is not its value is
not copied: either refuses the change, `unreadable`. The write reads the
row again by a hash of all of it. A fork's copy is made the same way: in
the change that makes the fork, each value the source reads now is opened
as its own rows label it, where it is held, a reference's source in another
project included, and sealed again as the new key's first version.

A value written with no nature is never looser than the key's was, as its
sealed row says, which is 0.14.7's rule outside a personal config: a
sensitive key's stays sensitive, and anything else is written a secret, a
plain one's included, so a value is plain only where its write says so. A
key the environment does not hold is what it inherits, as a use finds it:
the nearest parent's key by that name, unless a fork on the way dismissed
it, so a fork's value over a sensitive key of its parent's stays sensitive;
a key inherited from nowhere is a secret. An archived key brought back with
a value read its parent's until then, so it is as sensitive as either its
own or that one. A reference's takes its source's
nature; its source's row is never missing (`source_id` restricts its
deletion), so one the vault does not see refuses the write, `unreadable`, as
a parent, a key or a dismissal edited behind it does. So does the key's own
row, whatever the write says: it still holds the key's name, and the key is
not made again beside it. A nature said
is the one written, looser included: who may loosen a value in place comes
with the nature change.

`secret.write`'s entry gives each key's version and nature as written. It
does not say what the nature was before, as 0.14's did (`was`): that is in
the entry of the write before, and a version's row, under no MAC, is not
read for a decision but by a restore, which its opening holds to it.

In a fork, a key is reset to what a parent passes down by `secret.write`
too (`inherit: true`, 0.14's `inheritSecret`): its own row is archived,
with its history, and `inherit: false` brings it back over the parent's.
Either takes `secret.write`, as writing over a parent's does, since
neither loses a value, and undoing one `secret.read` too, as any return.
A key no live parent passes down is `not_inherited`, 409: that would be
an archiving, or its return, which take `secret.archive`. A reset is
refused while a reference reads the key, `referenced`, as an archive is.
A value, a version or a reference written over a reset key makes it an
override again with no `archived: false`, as 0.14 wrote over it; any
other archived key is refused, `archived`. Whatever makes a key hold a
value or a reference in a fork again ends its dismissal there, if any
(0.14's `endRemovals`), so that reset later, it reads its parent's.
Example: staging overrides prod's `DATABASE_URL`, and its Contributor,
who writes and does not archive, resets it: staging reads prod's value,
and its own is version history. They cannot undo it, since that serves a
value again that they do not read; a Developer can.

A personal config's keys (`mine`, `dev@me`) are written by `secret.write`
and `secret.refile` too, by its owner alone, a person, while they read or
use the environment it is a config of, and as far as their credential's
scope does there: a Viewer's session keeps a local value, and their
viewer's token writes nothing; a service account is `not_a_person`. A
write that gives a key a value makes the config where they have none, in
the same change, as `environment.create` makes one, so a refused write
leaves none, where 0.14 made it first, in a transaction of its own.
Anything else asked of a config not there is asked of a key not there:
`unknown_key`, or `no_folder`. A config holds no reference, and a value
typed there with no nature over a plain one, its own or the one it reads
now, stays plain (0.14.7's `bareLevel`): nobody but its owner lists it.

A reference is a key whose row names another's, `source_id`: `secret.write`
makes one, with `ref`, and `reference.break` ends one. Who pointed it there,
and when, are its row's too, `source_set_by` and `source_set_at`, under its
MAC: set as `source_id` is set or pointed elsewhere (by `secret.write`, or a
fork as references, by its forker), and cleared with it, when a value is
written over it, it is broken, or a place's deletion ends it. A check holds
the three together. The member report lists by them what someone lent: a
reference goes on lending its source's value after its maker loses access
to the source. Making one hands the
source's value to whoever reads the key's environment, as it changes, so its
maker writes there, reads the source where it is held, and uses it there
too if it is sensitive. A workload refers only to a value it is handed back
where it is held: a plain one, or one its binding lists. The source is a
live key of a live environment, no personal config's, and is no reference
itself; nor is a key that live references read made one, so a read is
one hop. Each is decided on what the write leaves. Example: one write
makes `NEW` a reference to `DATABASE_URL` and gives `DATABASE_URL` a
sensitive value: it is refused to a maker who reads `DATABASE_URL` but
does not use it, as making `DATABASE_URL` a reference or archiving it in
the same write is to anyone. A key made a reference keeps its value as
history and has none of its own, so its next value is numbered after it.
Whoever writes the key breaks the reference, and so does whoever manages
access in its source's project, who decides who reads the source and need
not see the key's environment. A reference elsewhere whose row the vault
does not see blocks nothing, as for an archive: a writer who hid it gains
nothing, since a read through two references is refused, `unreadable`.
0.14 sealed each reference in the log and ended it `replaced` or
`abandoned` by calls of their own; under the row's MAC, a value written
over a reference ends it, and a write refused leaves none to end.

A version is restored by `secret.write` too (`restore`, 0.14's
`restoreVersion`): the key's next version, its value the old one's,
opened and sealed again (above), and its first date the old one's. It
comes back as it was kept, plain, a secret or sensitive, as its version's
row labels it, and sensitive while the key is, as a value written with no
nature finds it, or while its newest version's row says its value was,
which a key made a reference keeps. That row carries no MAC, so the engine
opens it as it is labelled before the write, as it opens a copy, and a
newest version relabelled refuses the restore, `unreadable`. Example:
`TOKEN` is plain at version 1, sensitive at version 2, then made a
reference; someone sets version 2's row to not sensitive; a restore of
version 1 is refused rather than written plain. A write counts each version
it restores as the most a value weighs, 192 KiB, against its 1 MiB, since
the vault reads the value only after that check: 5 restores at most, so a
write never loads more than a few MiB of stored values. Made by a recipe or in a pair, it comes
back as typed, where 0.14 kept the recipe: Regenerate would make a value
by a recipe the restored one was not made by, and a half would pair with
one it no longer matches. Whoever restores writes and reads the key there
(`secret.write`, `secret.read`), as a return asks: the old value serves
again. 0.14 asked `secret.use` of a sensitive one and nothing of a plain
one. A version the key's row does not count is `unknown_version`; an
archived key is brought back first, in the same write or before. Example:
Ada restores version 1 of `STRIPE_KEY`, a secret, over version 3,
sensitive: version 4 is version 1's value, sensitive, first written on
version 1's day. A value written again as sensitive is a new value to the
vault: an older version of the same text comes back as it was kept, where
0.14 knew it by its ciphertext, which a restore no longer copies.

A value a client generated (`generated`, `@coffre/core/recipe`) is written
by `secret.write` with the recipe it was made by, as `formatRecipe` writes
it, on the key's row, under its MAC, which Regenerate makes the next one by.
A key pair's two halves are written together, each naming both, under one
id the vault draws, each its half; a pair in half, a recipe not as it is
kept, or a pair named by a recipe that makes one value is a fault, which
the app refuses first, 400, with 0.14.7's words. A value typed over either
half, or either archived, leaves the other the half of a pair that is no
more, which the listing shows as typed, as 0.14's did. A generated value
is never plain: written with no nature, a secret, or sensitive over a
sensitive one, a personal config's plain one included, where a typed one
stays plain. A regenerate says the version each key's value `replaces`, by
the id the listing gave (`versionId`): the vault writes it only over the
version the key reads now, its own live one or, in a fork that holds none
live and did not dismiss it, the one it inherits, as their sealed rows say,
and anything else is `stale` (409), the whole write refused. Example: Bo
lists `TOKEN` at version 3 and regenerates it; Ada wrote version 4 between
the two; the regenerate is refused rather than write over a value Bo never
saw. `replaces: null` says the key reads no value now and is no reference
(new, a reference broken, or archived with nothing passed down);
`replaces: { source }`, that it reads a reference to that secret, by the id
the listing gives (`reference.sourceId`). Anything else refuses either the
same: an MCP approval's write, which asks the vault through no route, says
one for a key its page showed without a version. Example: the page showed
`NEW_KEY` absent, and Bo made it a reference to `API_KEY` before Approve's
write; the write says `null`, and is `stale`. A dry run of a generated value is
`changed`, never compared: it is new by its making, as 0.14's dry run said. A version's id is under no MAC
([What a version's row holds](#data-structures)): a database writer who
gives version 4's row version 3's id makes that regenerate go through,
which writes nothing its writer may not write anyway. Accepted, as 0.14.7
had it.

A key is archived, brought back and filed by `secret.write` too, beside
any value. A folder of an environment's keys renamed or emptied is one
change, `secret.refile`: every key filed there, archived ones included,
by whoever writes keys there, as filing one key takes; a folder no key is
filed in is `no_folder`. A row's folder is known only once its MAC holds,
and a row edited behind the vault may have been filed there or moved out,
so the vault reads the environment's keys whole, not by the folder, and
one row that does not hold refuses the refile, `unreadable`, rather than
leave its key in a folder renamed. 0.14 logged one `secret.move` a key;
the entry here names the keys.

A missing key is dismissed, or restored, by `missing.dismiss`: every key
named in one change, by whoever writes keys there, and one entry naming
those dismissed and those restored, where 0.14 logged one a key (0.14's
`setDismissals`). Each dismissal is a sealed row, kept when restored
(`restored_at`), since in a fork, or a personal config (`dev@me`), it is
also a removal: the environment no longer inherits the key, and a use
there goes without it. So a key the environment holds a live row of, a
value or a reference, is not dismissed in a fork, `held_here` (409): it
would serve whatever is dismissed, and reset to what it inherits first,
or archived, it can be. Outside a fork, as 0.14.7 did, such a dismissal
is moot, and allowed. A personal config is dismissed in by its owner
alone, once they have one: a dismissal makes none, `no_config` (404),
where 0.14.7 answered 404 too. One change names 2,000 keys at most, as
any body the app takes; more is `too_many_dismissals`, refused once the
caller is known to write there, with none of the keys read. Example:
EU, a fork of prod, dismisses `SENTRY_DSN`: EU's missing keys list it
apart, a use of EU goes without it, and so does a fork of EU; restored, EU
reads prod's again. EU dismissing its own `DATABASE_URL` is `held_here`,
until it is reset to prod's.

A key is renamed by `secret.rename`, with its history, by whoever writes
keys there, as filing one takes: no value moves. It is a live key, and
its new name one its environment holds nowhere, archived or not
(`duplicate_key`); its references read it by its id still. The name
carries down to every environment below, forks of forks, archived ones
and personal configs included, in the same change (forking.md, "A key is
renamed in the parent"): each one's override takes the new name, and so
does its dismissal, so each goes on overriding or doing without the key
it did. A fork that holds the new name of its own refuses the rename,
`duplicate_key_below`, where 0.14 named the fork; a personal config is
named to nobody but its owner, so its own key of the new name is moved
out of the way, `NEW_2`, `_3` and on, a name nothing it holds or reads
has. Postgres checks a unique name row by row, in no set order, so that
key gives up its name first: a change's write may go `first`, before any
other (`aside` in `packages/vault/src/change.ts`), whose statements each
wait on its count. Every row the cascade decides on is read by either
name, or a name a key moved aside could take; one whose MAC does not
hold refuses the rename, `unreadable`, rather than leave a fork behind.
So does any environment of the project: the forks below and the parents
above are found by `parent_id`, and a parent cut loose behind the vault
would hide its keys from the names a key moved aside avoids, then shadow
one once put back.
Example: prod's `STRIPE_KEY` becomes `STRIPE_SECRET_KEY`; staging's
override follows, EU's dismissal follows, and Ada's config, which
overrode `STRIPE_KEY` and held a `STRIPE_SECRET_KEY` of her own, now
overrides `STRIPE_SECRET_KEY` with her old override, and keeps her own
key as `STRIPE_SECRET_KEY_2`.

**A place deleted for good** (`project.delete`, `environment.delete`:
`placeDelete` in `packages/vault/src/ops/places.ts`) is 0.14.7's
deletion, in one change. 0.14 revoked the grants by a vault call per
member, ended the references by another, then erased and renamed in an
app transaction that checked again under the head; a deletion cut off
between them finished when asked again. Here one read sees all of it and
one statement writes it: a row that moved since the read starts the
change over, as any change's does. It is asked of an archived project, or
an environment archived itself (`not_archived`), by an Admin or Owner of
all the instance on no credential's scope (`runsInstance`); a fork,
archived or not, keeps its parent (`inherited`); an environment's personal
configs go with it. A personal config alone is deleted by its owner, or
by whoever manages its project's environments, archived or not.

What goes:
- **Values.** Every version under the place that still holds one has its
  `ciphertext` and `wrapped_dek` emptied and its `plaintext` cleared, by
  one `UPDATE` in the change's statement (`Plan.erase` in
  `packages/vault/src/change.ts`), however many there are: the plan names
  the places, never the versions, which a version's row needs no MAC for.
  With no wrapped key, a value opens under no vault key.
- **Grants** on the place, lapsed ones too: their rows dropped.
- **References** into it and out of it: each holder's `source_id` cleared.
  A reference holds no value of its own (its `current_version` is null),
  so the holder reads nothing, `no_value`, and never the value it held
  before it pointed elsewhere, which stays history. One left pointing in
  by a row edited behind the vault reads nothing either: its source's
  place is no live place (`unreadable`).

What stays, names and dates only, because the log names them: the place's
row, under a tombstone's slug, `market~deleted-2026-11-14` (`-2`, `-3`
the same day), sealed again; its keys' rows, untouched; its versions'
rows, with their number, IV, tag, vault key's id, author and dates; its
dismissals. A slug with a `~` is no place: no use, change, restore,
rename, grant or reference reaches into it, and its old slug is free.
Backups taken before still hold the encrypted values.
Example: prod holds `API_KEY`, `copied` is a fork that copied it,
`referred` one that holds references to it, and billing's `LINK` was a
reference to it once, broken since. Prod deleted, `copied` reads its
`API_KEY` still, a value of its own sealed when it was made; `referred`'s
`API_KEY` reads nothing, while its `PAY`, a reference to billing's
`STRIPE`, reads on.

How much it takes is counted beside its read (`Operation.beside`), under
no MAC, for its answer and its bound only, and counted again under the
change lock with the rows it read: a personal config is deleted live, so
its owner may write to it meanwhile, and a count that moved starts the
deletion over, which then says, erases and bounds what is there. Past
`MOST_ERASED`, 20,000 versions holding a value, it is refused,
`too_large`, and nothing is erased: a project's environments are deleted one at a time first. Its
write is one statement, which `WRITE_MS` holds to five seconds; 20,000
versions are erased in about 300 ms on a local Postgres. It writes one row
for the place (and one for each personal config it takes), one for each
grant and each reference, and the versions by that one `UPDATE`; it reads
the place's environments, grants and references, never its versions.
A fork's copy is bounded the same way: a fork copies 2,000 values at most
(`MOST_KEYS`, the most one write seals), and past that it is refused,
`too_many_keys`, whole; by reference, or inheriting, it seals none.

Nothing but a change writes what a MAC covers. A use moves a row's last-seen
columns on, one row a statement, and takes no lock. The signer's upkeep
deletes rows through changes like any other.
Example: three Owners each demote themselves at once. Each read three Owners
and each decision is right alone. The first write changes an Owner row the
other two counted, so they start again, and the last one is refused.

**Signing in** is a change, with one difference: nobody is asking yet.
Three operations have no caller: a sign-in, the start of a device login,
and the CLI collecting its session. Each is sent with where its request
came from and no token (`Trace`: its id and its address, for the log), and
reads no caller's rows.

*What the app is trusted for.* The vault has no way out to GitHub or to an
OIDC provider, so the app runs the provider's flow and tells the vault the
account it verified: `{ provider, issuer, subject, emails }`, the emails
the provider verified, its primary one first. The vault takes that on the
app's word. It is the one place the app says who someone is. An app that
is taken over can therefore sign in as anyone; what it cannot do is hide
it, since each sign-in is an entry under the vault's MAC that names the
account. A passkey, once ported, is checked by the vault itself: only it
can trust the passkey's row.

*Tokens.* The app draws each token, 32 random bytes after its kind's
prefix, and sends its SHA-256 with the change that makes the credential.
It hands the token to whoever asked and keeps nothing. The vault never
sees a token, here as everywhere.

*How long a session lasts* is the deployment's sign-in configuration, which
the app holds (`browserSessionHours`, 12 unless told; `cliSessionDays`,
30). The app sends the minutes, and the vault refuses more than that
configuration could ever say: 7 days for a browser, 365 for a CLI.

| Operation | Asked by | Reads | Writes | Entry |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `signin` | Nobody: an account the app verified | The account's identity and its member; the member each email names, and their accounts; the session the browser showed, if any | The identity, new or signed in again; a browser session; the session it replaces, ended; an Owner the configuration names, made or made one again | `sign_in`, saying whether it bound the account or made an Owner |
| `account.link` | A person's browser session, with a second account the app verified | The caller; that account's identity | The identity | `account.link` |
| `account.unlink` | The caller, of one of their accounts | The caller; their identities | The identity, revoked | `account.unlink` |
| `credential.end` | The caller: the credential they came in on (signing out), another of theirs, a token of a service account they manage, or anyone's for whoever runs the instance | The caller; the credential; for a service's token, what says who manages it | The credential, revoked | `sign_out`, `token.revoke` |
| `token.create` | The caller: a personal token, a handle, a service account's token | The caller; the places its scope names; for a service's, the service and what says who manages it | The credential | `token.create` |
| `device.start` | Nobody: a CLI | The row that holds the user code drawn, if one does | The device login | `device.start` |
| `device.decide` | A person's session, a browser's or a CLI's, by the user code | The caller; the device login | Its decision | `device.approve`, `device.deny` |
| `device.redeem` | Nobody: the CLI, by its device code's hash | The device login; whoever approved it | It, consumed; a CLI session | `sign_in` |

*A sign-in's decision*, 0.14's:

1. The account is bound: its identity, found by provider, issuer and
   subject, is not revoked. Its member signs in, if they are active and of
   the generation the identity was bound in. Otherwise `deactivated`.
2. The account is new: the first of its emails that names a member,
   `user:<email>`, names who it is. That member must be active
   (`deactivated`) and have no account yet, none that is not revoked and
   of their generation: one who signs in with another already is refused
   `account_mismatch`, and links this one from a session of theirs.
   Otherwise the account is bound to them and they sign in. No email names
   a member: `not_registered`.
3. An email the vault's configuration names an Owner (`owners`) is a
   member whatever the rows say: with no row they are made one, and one who
   is not an active Owner of the whole instance is made one again, in the
   same change. This is how an Owner added to the configuration later gets
   in; the others were made at adoption.

Example: Ada, invited as `ada@acme.example`, signs in with GitHub for the
first time. The app verifies her account and sends `signin` with her
emails and the hash of a token it drew. The vault finds no identity for
that GitHub subject, finds her member's row by her email and no account of
hers, and writes two rows in one statement: her identity and her session.
The app sets the cookie. Next week the same change finds the identity and
writes the session, and the identity's last sign-in.

A row written behind the vault is not a row that is absent. A sign-in
decides on rows that are not there: no identity means the account is new,
no member's row means the next email is tried, no account of theirs means
this one may be bound. So when its read finds, among the rows its path
decides on, one with a MAC that is not the vault's, the sign-in is refused
`tampered`, and the signer's next check names the row. For an account
that is bound, those rows are the account and its member; for a new one,
also the members its emails name and their accounts. The read finds a
member's accounts revoked or not, so that one marked revoked behind the
vault is found.
Example: Bo signs in with GitHub. A writer marks his identity's row
revoked, then signs in with an Okta account that verified Bo's address.
Without the rule the vault would see a member with no account, and bind
it.

What the rule does not reach is a row taken out of the read altogether:
Bo's identity deleted, or pointed at another member, for the one sign-in,
and put back after. That takes a database writer who also holds another
account at any provider the deployment trusts, verified for Bo's address, and
who takes away with the identity every session it opened that still has a
row: a foreign key holds them to it. The binding it makes stays, in a kept
entry and on Bo's account page. It is the gap "a row taken away for one
decision" above, accepted there; closing it here would take a count of a
member's accounts on their own sealed row.

A refused sign-in writes nothing and is not logged: nobody who stands asked
it (D236). The refusal is bare. 0.14 told someone refused
`account_mismatch` which providers they sign in with; the page now says to
sign in with the account they have and link this one.

*Last sign-in* is a fact a sign-in writes, so `identities.last_sign_in_at`
is under its row's MAC like any other column. Only a credential's and a
binding's last use stay outside it, which a use moves on.

*What ends without a write.* A removal moves a member's generation on, and
an unlinking revokes an identity: every credential of the older generation,
and every session the account opened, is refused from then on by the rule
that reads them (`callerOf`). 0.14 wrote each of those rows. `credential.end`
writes the one row it is asked to end. Signing out with a token the vault
does not know is refused `unauthenticated`, which the app answers as signed
out all the same.

A removal is to revoke the member's accounts too, a row or two (not built
yet: it comes with the members' operations). An account is bound to one
member at a time, which a unique index holds, and a row of an older
generation would hold it for good: the member, let back in, could never
sign in with it again. Revoked, it is free, and their next sign-in binds
it anew by their email, as a first one does. The upgrade revokes the ones
0.14 left live.

A sign-in is the one change that can be asked of a database the vault has
not adopted: every other needs a caller, whose rows the adoption sealed.
So it reads the instance's policy row, which the adoption writes, and is
refused `adopting` without it (`tampered`, if the row is there and not the
vault's). Until the adoption's first entry is signed
the vault is to answer every call 503 (see the upgrade's steps), which is
the host's to hold and not built yet.

*A device login* is `coffre login`'s (RFC 8628), on a laptop or a server
reached over SSH. The app draws two codes: the device code, which only the
CLI holds and the vault has by its SHA-256, and the user code, eight
letters, `BCDF-GHJK`, which a person types in their browser. Each waits 10
minutes. `device.start` writes the row, and is refused `taken` when a row
holds either code, which the app answers by drawing again. A person decides
it on a session of theirs, as 0.14.7 let them, and once: of two deciding at
once, the second finds it decided. The CLI polls `device.redeem` with its
device code, and is told, writing nothing, `pending`, `denied` or `expired`
(RFC 8628's three) until it collects its session, once: the row consumed
and a CLI session of the approver's, in one statement. Its decision rests
on that row and the approver's: an approval of a member removed or let back
in since collects nothing, and either row written behind the vault refuses
it, `tampered`, since a row that says someone approved would become their
session. A decision and a collection each hold until five seconds
before the code expires (`Plan.until`, less `WRITE_MS`, the time a write
is given, which a row lock may take whole): a write that would start
after that is decided again, and refused. So the code's last five seconds
decide and collect nothing.
Example: Ada runs `coffre login` on a server. Its CLI starts a device
login and shows `BCDF-GHJK`; Ada types it in her browser and approves.
The CLI's next poll collects a session of Ada's, labelled as the CLI
called itself. Had a database writer marked the row approved in Root's
name, the poll would have been refused `tampered`.

What changed from 0.14.7: a collection refused because its approver was
removed consumes nothing, and is not logged, as no refused change nobody
asks is (D236); 0.14.7 consumed the row, and logged one of its two such
exits. Starting one is logged, `device.start`, with no actor.

*Built so far:* `signin`, `credential.end`, `account.link`, `account.unlink`,
`token.create`, `device.start`, `device.decide`, `device.redeem`.

*A cap on strangers is the app's.* 0.14 let 5 device logins wait per
address and 200 in all. That protects the service from a flood and
nobody's access: a row deleted behind the vault lets one more code be asked
for, and nothing else. So the app counts them, one statement on an index,
before it asks the vault, and `device.start` reads only whether the user
code it drew is taken. So is a binding's rate, below. A cap that decides
access stays the vault's: the keys a principal reads in fifteen minutes.
The app counts by its own clock, given to the statement, not the
database's `now()`: the vault dates a code's expiry on its clock, the app's
on the same platform, and the database's may be minutes off both, which at
ten minutes ahead would count no code at all. The page that shows a code
reads it the same way. A skew between app and vault moves where the cap and
the page draw the line by as much, and nothing more: the vault decides on
its own clock.

*The browser's round trip.* What a sign-in carries through the provider and
back (the state, the PKCE verifier, where to land, an account being linked)
stays a cookie the app seals with its own key. That is all the app key does
for people's sign-in: it signs no row.

*What changed from 0.14.* 0.14 ended a browser session when its provider's
issuer was no longer the configured one, a tenant moved after a compromise
say. 0.16 does not look: an identity is found by its issuer, so nobody
signs in through the old one again, and the sessions it opened end when
they expire, within 12 hours unless the deployment says longer. A CLI's
session never named an account, in 0.14 either.

*Who gives a service a whole project, as 0.14.7.* A person who does not
manage access on a project gives a service account a role on all of it,
and so manages one that holds such a grant and makes its tokens, only
holding each of the role's environment permissions in each environment it
protects now (rule 4 of [roles](roles.md)): a Developer of a project with
nothing protected gives a service Developer on all of it, and once prod is
protected, environment by environment. The rows that say which are
protected are the decision's, so one hidden from it goes unweighed
(D241).

**A workload signing in** is two more changes nobody asks, and one its
session asks. A CI run trades its platform's ID token for a five-minute
token of its service account. A workload trades a proof (an ID token, or a
request AWS signed) for a session bound to a key it holds, and renews it.

*What the app verifies, and what the vault decides.* A proof is checked
where it can be. The request's own proof of its key (RFC 9449) is the
app's: it sees the request, and sends the key's thumbprint with every
call, as built. A platform's proof needs the platform: its signing keys,
fetched, or AWS asked. So the app does one thing with an ID token: it
verifies the signature. It hands the vault the token itself, the keys it
verified it under (the issuer and the keys' address), and its own public
URL, which a token's audience names. For a request AWS signed, the app
checks the request whole, asks AWS, and hands over AWS's answer: the
account, the role and the user. The vault takes the signature, or AWS's
answer, on the app's word, as it takes an account at sign-in. Where a
binding carries its keys (Kubernetes), the vault needs no word at all: the
app sends the token and its issuer, and the vault verifies the signature
itself, under the keys on its own sealed row.

That check is the one step of a change that is neither its read nor its
decision. A decision is synchronous and reads nothing, and verifying a
signature is neither on Workers. So an operation may name a `check`: run
between the read and the decision, on the rows read, with no transaction
open, its result handed to the decision, and run again each time the
change starts over. It reads nothing and writes nothing.

Everything else of 0.14's verification read the binding's row, and the
app cannot check a row's MAC. So the vault does it, on its own rows and
its own clock, with the same pure functions moved to `@coffre/core`
(`packages/core/src/identity/proofs.ts`, `aws.ts` and `profiles.ts` beside it):

- it reads the claims from the token, and hashes what was signed;
- of the service's bindings that have the keys the app verified under
  (for one that carries its keys, those on the issuer the app named) and
  take the token's audience (a binding's policy's, the key's thumbprint
  where it asks for one, its profile's, or the instance's), the oldest
  whose claims match. Which audience is tried is 0.14's rule: the
  token's own if some binding takes it, otherwise the instance's;
- the token's age and lifetime, by its profile, and the profile's own
  rules (Azure's version, Kubernetes' subject);
- for a session: its binding's sources, against the request's address,
  and whether the deployment lets a binding list reads, as 0.14 refused
  the sign-in itself;
- whether the proof is one that is spent; and the session's hard end, the
  earliest of its binding's `sessionHours` and, unless the token names
  the key, the token's own expiry.

Example: a binding takes Azure tokens whose audience is `api://coffre-prod`.
A writer sets its row's audience to another API the same identity calls,
for a moment, and presents a token for that API, which the API's operator
holds. The signature is Microsoft's, and the app says so. The vault reads
its row: put back by then, it names `api://coffre-prod`, and the audience
is refused; still edited, it is not the vault's, and the exchange is
refused `tampered`. Had the app checked the audience, on the row as it
read it, the session would have been made.

A binding whose row is there and not the vault's refuses the exchange
(`tampered`), as at sign-in: the oldest match wins, and one passed over
would let a later one answer.

A refused exchange is told why, in the app's words: 0.14 named the claims
that differed from the nearest binding, and the app still can, from the
token and its own read of the bindings, a listing that decides nothing.
Too many at once, or the issuer's keys not answering, are the app's own
429 and 503, before the vault is asked.

*Built so far:* `token.exchange`, `workload.signin` and `workload.renew`
(`packages/vault/src/ops/workloads.ts`). A deployment that takes no
workloads, or only key-bound sessions (`useOnly`), is refused `no_match` at
the exchange too, behind the app's 404 and 410; one that takes no workloads
makes no session either.

*Built so far, too:* `token.bind` and `token.unbind`
(`packages/vault/src/ops/bindings.ts`), 0.14's `bind`, `replaceKeys` and
`unbind`, one entry each: a binding made and those it replaces revoked,
or each binding on an issuer replaced by one with the keys uploaded, is
one `token.bind`. Who binds, and what a binding reaches, is the rule of
a service's token (`token.create`: `managesService`, `issuesCredential`,
`ownsProject`); the vault adds none. The scope is always sealed on the
row: asked, or what the bindings it replaces reached and the service
still holds (each grant no wider than it was, `narrowest`), or all the
service holds. Example: `ci` holds Developer on `market/dev` and Viewer
on `market/prod`, and a binding of it reached `market/dev` as Viewer;
Ada, an Owner of market, binds again with no scope, replacing it, and
the new binding reaches `market/dev` as Viewer, reading its sensitive
values only if the old one did. Not yet: 0.14's dry run, which answered
what a binding would reach and wrote nothing, and its list of the places
whose sensitive values a binding will not read (`withheld`): both are the
change weighed (`weigh`), its answer grown by what the decision already
knows.

*Served over HTTP:* the bindings' four routes (`api/routes/bindings.ts`)
and a CI run's exchange, `POST /api/auth/oidc` (`exchange.ts`).
`GET /members/:member/bindings` is one `caller` and one statement, which
reads the service, its grants and the instance's setting to weigh whether
the caller manages it (`managesService`, as the vault weighs it, on rows
it does not check: a listing), and the bindings with the names of the
places and keys they name; it lists to the service itself and its
managers. Making, re-keying and removing one is one `change` and no
statement. The app asks the issuer where its keys are
(`discoverKeys`), only once that same weighing says the caller manages
the service, so that no one else makes coffre fetch a host they name:
one `caller` and one statement more, on that path alone. It checks the binding's shape and its profile's and
policy's rules first, since the vault takes a binding of another shape as
a fault: each mistake is a 400 that says why, with nothing asked. A
binding made answers its id and what it reaches by path, and those it
replaced (0.14.7 answered the whole binding); re-keyed, each binding made
in the order of those it replaced. `?dryRun=1` is 501 until the dry run
is built. The exchange costs its admission, one statement (the service's
live bindings on the token's issuer, which say whose keys to verify
under) and one `change`; a token none can take costs no vault call. 0.14's
count of what one binding issues a minute (60) is not kept yet: the
service's gate keeps it, with the workload's sign-in. Until then, an
unknown key's alert is capped once an hour per service in each isolate
or process, not once per deployment as 0.14.7 capped it in the database,
and each refusal is one log line a minute per reason and service. Nor are
`/api/auth/workload` and `/api/auth/workload/renew` served yet.

*A session's request* (`packages/server/src/sessions.ts`) comes as
`Authorization: DPoP coffre_wl_…`, with a proof its key signed for it.
Before the vault hears of it, the app parses the proof (no I/O), reads
the session's row by the token's hash for its service and its key (one
statement, unchecked), verifies the proof under that key for the
request's method, URL and token, and has the service's gate take its
nonce and its `jti`. Only then is the key's thumbprint the request's
`proof`, and the vault decides on its own rows. Every answer past the
gate carries its nonce (`DPoP-Nonce`). A session's API call costs that
statement, one call to the gate and the route's own vault call and
statements; 0.14 paid one statement warm and four cold, and asked the
vault only cold. A refusal costs at most the statement and the gate:
no proof, or one that does not parse, reads nothing; another key's, or
one made for another request, reads the row and asks no gate; a replay,
or a nonce the gate no longer holds (`use_dpop_nonce`, with the one to
sign with), asks no vault. A session's token as a bearer token is 401
`workload`, with nothing read. A deployment that binds no `GATES`
(`gates: env.GATES`, `export const CoffreGates = coffre.Gates`) takes no
session: 503 `no_gate`, since nothing would stop a replay.

A session past the gate that the vault stops, at its caller, a use or a
change alike, is answered as 0.14.7 answered it, not by the table in
"What a refusal becomes", which is a person's: `pending` and `source` are
401 `unauthenticated`, and `narrowed`, `no_reads` and `frozen` 403
`forbidden`, each with its reason and 0.14.7's words (`198.51.100.4 is
outside the binding's sources`). A person's `no_reads`, a binding that
lists reads where the instance lets none, stays 400.

Example: a deploy job signed in as `deploy` sends `GET /api/me` with a
proof that carries no nonce. It gets 401 `use_dpop_nonce` and `deploy`'s
gate's nonce, `g12.…`, signs again with it, and gets its answer and the
nonce to sign the next with. The same proof sent again is 401
`replayed`; a proof of the job's key sent with `api`'s token is 401
`jkt`. Someone who edits `deploy`'s row to name their own key gets their
proof past the gate, and the vault refuses it: its row's MAC no longer
holds.

A session's row carries its binding's scope and the environments it
reaches as of its sign-in, by id, which the vault reads from the projects
the bindings' scopes name: each environment a grant names, and each of a
project it names but personal configs, live, in a live project (0.14's
`environmentsOf`). A place among them that is not the vault's refuses the
sign-in (`tampered`), since that list chooses where `/use/<credential>`
goes when it names none.

Example: `api` signs in to a binding whose scope is `market` as `viewer`;
`market` has `prod`, `staging`, an archived `old` and Ada's personal
config of prod. Its session lists prod and staging. With a token whose
`exp` is in 4 minutes and no key in its audience, the session ends in 4
minutes, renewal or not; with STS's answer and `sessionHours: 2`, it
expires in an hour, renews an hour at a time, and ends at 2 hours.

What 0.14.7 declared and never served is not built here either: a session
that waits for approval, enrollment codes, narrowed sessions, freezes of a
service or a binding, and a call over a Cloudflare service binding. The
vault reads those rows where they exist, and no operation writes them.
The service binding's caller has no token and no proof: the platform hands
coffre what the calling Worker's own binding declares, so whoever deploys
Workers on the account can name any service. It comes with the 0.15
line's features, with that written down.

*Which stops a session's own changes ignore.* A session that stands and is
stopped (outside its binding's sources, frozen, waiting) opens nothing.
Its renewal ignores a freeze, and only a freeze, as in 0.14, so that
lifting the freeze restores it; and it signs itself out whatever stops it
(`credential.end`).

*A proof is spent once.* The table of spent proofs is the one a change
writes that carries no MAC and that no decision reads: `spent_id_tokens`,
a hash and the date it was spent. A plan names the hash it spends
(`once`). The write inserts it only if the rows read have not moved, and
makes the change only if the hash was not there: so a change that starts
over does not find its own hash, and one that finds it is the refusal
`replayed`, which writes nothing. The signer's minute deletes a hash two
hours after it was spent, past any token's hour. A row deleted behind the
vault lets a proof be traded again while the vault's own rule on its age
still takes it, by whoever holds it: an hour at most, and two entries
then carry one hash.

*A binding's last use* is a date no MAC covers, so it is no row the
exchange writes. Its plan names the binding it read as the one used, and
the change's statement moves that date on to the entry's, with everything
else or not at all: an exchange refused, replayed or started over is no
use. It costs no round trip and waits for nothing: only changes write
`bindings`, one at a time. A later date the row has stays. The operation
itself still only reads and decides; no operation holds the database.

*Renewal* takes the vault's clock, not the database's as 0.14's did: the
vault dates what it seals, and it is the vault that reads the expiry.
Its decision holds until 5 seconds before that expiry, and a write that
comes later is made over, as "Time changes no row" above says.
0.14 logged no renewal; in 0.16 it is a change, and so an entry, among
those that age out.

*What the app reads of a workload, and decides nothing on.* The app has no
session of its own to look up, with one exception: for a handle or a
workload's session it routes, it reads the credential's row, and for an
exchange the service's bindings, with no way to check their MACs. It uses
them to find things, never to decide access:

- *the gate.* One Durable Object per service, as 0.14 has (in process on
  Node). The row says which service a session is and which key it is
  bound to. The app refuses a proof signed by another key, then has the
  gate hold the proof to its nonce, refuse a replay and count the
  service's requests, before it asks the vault: a token without its key
  learns nothing and uses up no count, and nothing is opened or logged
  for a request the gate refuses. Every request of a session goes
  through it, a renewal and a sign-out too. A gate's nonces are its own
  and random, and a proof is taken only by the gate that issued its
  nonce: that is what lets an unverified row route it. A proof captured
  and sent to another service's gate carries a nonce that gate never
  issued.
- *the place.* `/use/<credential>` with no environment goes to the one
  place the credential was made for, which its row lists (a session's, as
  of its sign-in); several is 0.14's 409 that lists them. The app then
  asks the vault for a use at that place, by name, and the vault decides
  on the places it sealed on the credential. A row edited to list one of
  several places the credential was made for chooses among places it may
  use anyway, by naming one in its URL: no more than the intact database
  gives, so no `sole` flag holds the vault to the row's choice. Accepted.
- *a tunnel's end,* the credential's expiry.
- *the keys to verify under,* for an exchange: the newest binding's.
- *a binding's rate* (0.14: 60 a minute, counted under the log's head): a
  cap on a flood. The gate counts the sessions and tokens the vault made,
  by the binding its answer names, and the app checks the count of the
  binding its own read predicts before it asks. An attempt the vault
  refuses counts for nothing, so a stranger with genuine tokens of their
  own repository cannot use up a service's minute.

A row edited behind the vault can send a request to the wrong gate or
make the app ask about another place. The vault's answer is the same: it
decides on its own rows, for the place named.

A workload's alerts, and the hourly cap on an unknown key's, which were
log entries and `capped_alerts`, go to the function the deployment's
configuration names, capped in the service's gate.

**An MCP connection** (`packages/vault/src/ops/mcp.ts`) is a person's
approval of an MCP client, as in 0.14 (docs/design/mcp.md): Ada, on her
browser session, lets Claude read her secrets' names. It is a credential of
kind `mcp`, hers, and each access token Claude shows `/mcp` is another, of
an hour, under it: `ancestors` names the connection, as a `narrow` child
names its session. 0.14's access token was a MAC under the app's key with no
row; the vault finds a caller only by a row, and an app that vouched for the
connection it verified would make its key a credential again.

| Row | Kind | `token_hash` | `previous_token_hash` | `code_hash` | `ancestors` | Expires |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| The connection, its code waiting | `mcp` | the code's | null | the code's | null | in a minute (`CODE_SECONDS`) |
| The connection, redeemed | `mcp` | the refresh token's | the refresh token it last replaced | the code's | null | the CLI's days, from the redemption |
| An access token | `mcp` | its own | null | null | `[connection]` | in an hour, never past the connection |

Each row keeps the client as JSON in `client` (its id, host, how it is
known, its redirect and its scopes, the shape 0.14's connections were
carried over in), and the connection's name in `label`. An access token's
`client` says the scopes it holds, the connection's or fewer.

Four changes:

- `mcp.connect`, asked by a person on a session of theirs (`no_access` for
  any token, which would connect an app with all its person holds): the
  connection, with the code's hash and the client's PKCE challenge. 20
  connections at most (`too_many_connections`), a code waiting among them.
  A registered client (`dcr`) is held to its row: the redirect must be one
  it registered, on any loopback port, and the name is the row's. Kept for
  good. Its entry says how the client is known, a registration's id, which
  the vault drew, and the client's host if it reads as one: never the
  client's name, a metadata document's URL or a redirect, which whoever
  made the client chose (the log takes no free text from a caller). The
  connection's row keeps them.
- `mcp.token`, nobody's but whoever holds the code with its verifier, or the
  refresh token: the connection's row moves on to the next refresh token
  and an access token's row is made, in one statement. A code is good
  within its minute, a refresh within its connection's life, each less a
  write's time (`Plan.until`, as a device login's). Redeeming a code ends
  the person's earlier connections of the same client that it grants all
  of and more (a step-up, as 0.14.7). Its code, however many refreshes
  on, or the refresh token it last replaced, presented again is a copy
  someone else holds (OAuth 2.1, 4.3.1): the change ends the connection,
  and is made (`ended: true`), since a refusal writes nothing; the app
  answers `invalid_grant`. An older refresh token is unknown
  (`unknown_grant`) and ends nothing, as in 0.14.7: whoever holds it got
  nothing with it, and keeping each a connection replaced would be a row
  an hour for its life. The access
  tokens under it end with it, by the connection's row alone. Of two
  refreshes racing on one token, the second finds it replaced: one new
  token, or none, never two. An access token's coming ages out; a
  connection ended is kept.
- `mcp.revoke` (RFC 7009), nobody's but whoever holds the token: the
  client gives back its refresh token, or an access token, by its SHA-256,
  and the connection ends, with every access token under it, as a
  disconnect does. An access token finds its connection by its
  `ancestors`. A code waiting, a refresh token replaced, an access token
  that lapsed, another client's (when the client names itself) or a
  connection ended already end nothing (`unknown_grant`), which the
  endpoint answers 200 all the same. Kept, as every connection's end is.
- `oauth.register` (RFC 7591), nobody's: a client's row. What bounds it: the
  app's limiter, per source and in total, before it asks; then the table's
  ceiling, 10,000 rows (`MAX_CLIENTS`), the vault's or not, past which it
  is refused `full`. The read finds the ceiling by the last row the table
  may hold in its key's order, and the write reads that row again under the
  change lock, as every row a decision read: two registrations racing for
  the last place do not both have it. Its entry ages out, so a stranger
  does not grow what the log keeps for good; it names the client and
  counts its redirects, never its name or a redirect, a stranger's text.
  The upkeep deletes a
  registration no MCP credential names a week after it was made, as 0.14.7
  revoked it.

As a caller (`callerOf`), an access token is its person, of the generation
it was issued in, holding all they hold now, as their session does
(`credentialScope` null): no place scope. It stands while its connection
does, redeemed, not revoked, frozen or expired, of the same member and
generation; ending the connection (`DELETE /apps/:id` is `credential.end`)
or removing the person ends every token under it with no write of theirs.
The connection's own token, a code or a refresh token, is never a bearer.
`caller` answers its connection and scopes (`credential.connection`,
`credential.scopes`), for `/mcp` to say what the client may call, and its
use is its connection's (`last_used_at`), which Connected apps lists.

- A use hands a value to an MCP client only with the `reveal` scope
  (`mcp_no_reveal`).
- A change, or a weighing, from an MCP credential is refused, as a
  handle's is (`unauthenticated`): every change an MCP client asks for
  waits for its person, as in 0.14, and on Approve the page's request makes
  it on the person's browser session, the one that read what it does. So
  the vault decides no change on the connection's word. `write` and
  `manage-access` gate only whether the client may ask.

**The proxy** (the credential proxy and the other plugins, which stay the
app's) reaches values through `use` alone, as whoever its request is:

| 0.14, in process | 0.16 |
|---|---|
| `use(handle, …)`, the API's read under a handle | `use`, the handle's hash, purpose `use` or `expose`, the upstream credential's name |
| `use(workload, …)` | `use`, the session's hash and its key's thumbprint, once the app has held the proof to the request and its body |
| `handle(…)`: does it stand, when does it end, which places | The app's read of the credential's row, above. Whether it stands is the vault's answer to the use |
| `recheck`, a tunnel's | `use` again: one entry a minute at most |
| `exchange`, `signOut` | `token.exchange`, `credential.end` |
| `api(token)` | The API in process, as any request with that token |
| `logged` | The host a value was sent to and whether through the deployment's egress stay in a use's entry, as a hostname and a flag: the audit page says them. A presigned URL's path and a signature's digest go |

A use is logged once a minute for a key, a purpose and a version; with
the proxy, also for the upstream credential it was used for, so that one
key sent to two hosts within the minute is two entries, as in 0.14.

A workload's sign-in is answered with the places its session reaches, by
path. The vault answers them by id, as it sealed them on the session, and
the app names them from its own rows: a listing.

*One memory.* 0.14's proxy kept the values it opened itself, a handle's
and a workload's alike, for `cacheSeconds` (330 at Equisafe, 3600 at
most), and served them with no check that the credential still stood,
and through an outage of the database. That cache goes, and
`cacheSeconds` is read and ignored through 0.16. The vault's memory is
the only one (`memorySeconds`, 2 by default, 600 at most), and it is
every caller's: raising it delays a revocation for reveals, pages,
sessions and tunnels alike, where 0.14's cache delayed it for proxied
requests alone. So a proxied request costs one call to the vault, which
reads its rows again every two seconds. It fails with the database, within
the memory's length, unless the deployment sets `outageSeconds`: then it
is answered from the rows last read for it, during an outage alone (D238,
"Through an outage of the database"). `outageSeconds: 330` is what
Equisafe's `cacheSeconds: 330` becomes.

*A request through a credential* (`packages/server/src/plugins.ts`, the
host, and `plugins/credential-proxy.ts`):

- A handle on a URL that names its place: one `use`, no statement. On
  `/use/<credential>`: one statement first, the handle's row by its token's
  hash, for the places it was made for (`routedCredential`), unchecked.
- A workload's session: that statement, for its service, key and places,
  then its proof held to the request whole (`hdr`, `req`), then its
  service's gate, which takes the proof once and counts the credential's
  `limit` in the same call, then one `use`. A request the gate refuses
  asks the vault nothing.
- The app routes through an outage too: it keeps, for each credential, the
  row it read last (a thousand at most, until the credential ends, an hour
  at most), and uses it only when its read cannot reach the database. A
  failure is remembered two seconds, as the vault remembers one, so the
  requests in them wait for no timeout. The row says where to ask, and
  nothing the vault does not decide again.
- An upstream's answer is redacted of every value the use opened but the
  plain ones, which `use` says by each value's `nature`, the one it opened
  under.
- What the app alone can hold a session's request to, it checks on the
  vault's answer about the session: a narrowed session's methods and
  paths, and an unsigned upload, which only a binding that says
  `unsignedUploads` takes. The use is made and logged before either
  refuses; nothing goes upstream.
- `coffre run`'s plan of an environment (`GET /use/<project>/<env>`) is
  0.14.7's, on two calls and no statement. A `peek` first, purpose `use`:
  what the handle may use, each key's nature, nothing opened or logged.
  Then one `use`, purpose `expose`, of the keys the app is handed: plain
  ones, but a credential's whose rule would leave the handle no slot (S3's
  access key id), and the secrets no credential, signer or tunnel covers.
  So a covered secret is never opened, and never in the log. Each value is
  placed by the nature the `use` opened it under, never the peek's: a key
  the peek saw plain that is a secret by the time of the use stays its
  credential's placeholder, its value dropped, and the log says it was
  opened, which it was. A person who only uses the place, a Developer's
  protected prod, has the `use` refused (`no_access`) and gets the
  placeholders, every value in `left`. A JWT placeholder adds its
  credential's `use` (purpose `use`, for its claims) and the handle's row,
  for its end. The vault reads twice, the `use` naming its keys, so no ask
  the peek's rows answer, and writes once, with the handle's last use once
  a minute. Both calls ask `fresh`: they read now, never from the vault's
  memory. `coffre run` plans again right after it writes the values it
  generated, and a plan answered from rows read before that write would
  name the same keys to generate again, over versions the write replaced:
  each write `stale`, until the run gives up. A workload's
  plan is its gate, then one `use`, purpose `expose`, of every key: what
  it is not handed is `useOnly`.
- In an environment the deployment generates values for (`generate`), a
  person's plan reads their personal config of it (`market/local@me`, or
  the environment while they have none), and names in `generate` each key
  the app goes without whose current value a recipe made: its recipe, and
  the version it `replaces`, a listing's `versionId`. The peek says both,
  of a key the caller is given and no other, so a parent the person does
  not read tells neither (it is refused `parent_unreadable`), and the map
  costs nothing more. `coffre run` writes each, a secret, into the
  personal config (`PATCH /secrets/<project>/<env>@me`, as in 0.14.7), and
  of two runs at once one writes and the other is refused 409 `stale`. A
  key held in the person's own config sensitive is never named again
  (R18); a reference's recipe is its source's, never named; a recipe this
  release cannot make, never; nor a key pair's half, which is made only
  with its other half named, as 0.14.7 left it (`coffre generate
  --public-key`).
- Not served yet, 501 `not_implemented`: presigning, signing, verifying
  and a cipher's work (a presigned URL ends with the credential, which only
  the vault can say), tunnels, and a call through a Cloudflare service
  binding.

**The signer** runs in a Durable Object of the vault Worker, since it needs
the vault's keys to check an entry's MAC. It is a new object: the one 0.14
deployments have, `CoffreAnchor`, belongs to the app Worker and goes.

- **Each second, while entries wait:** move and number them, hold each change
  to the mirror, extend the tree, sign the head. A run that dies midway is
  finished by the next.
- **Each minute:** compare the rows with the mirror and each tree's size
  with its signed size, and re-check a slice of old entries. Then the upkeep,
  a bounded batch of each: uses past their keep, credentials a day past
  their expiry, one-shot rows that lapsed.
- **On a finding:** alert.

A run is three steps (`packages/vault/src/signer.ts`, step for step what
`log.qnt` models):

1. **Read**, one statement: the entries that wait, 256 at most, and on the
   minute's run every sealed row's MAC and each tree's last entry. The signer
   notes what it was told to expect before it reads. The statement selects
   the database's clock, as a use's read does, so that no pooler answers it
   from a cache.
2. **Sign**, with no database. It takes an entry whose MAC is the vault's,
   that it does not remember signing, dated within its window. It holds each
   change to the mirror, numbers what it took and extends the two trees. It
   writes what it decided to its own storage before anything moves.
3. **Move**, one statement: each entry into `log` if it still waits exactly
   as it was signed, its `log_index` rows, the tiles and the head. A run that
   died after step 2 is finished by the next signer, and running the
   statement twice changes nothing. A row someone planted where an entry was
   about to go does not stop it: the entry is reported `lost`, and the signer
   goes on.

One run at a time: a second is refused, since two that read the same entries
would each sign them. What a run finds is in the signer's storage with its
decision, and stays until whoever sends alerts takes it, whether the run
ended or failed.

Each statement waits at most 2 seconds for a lock, as a change does, and runs
at most 30. Whoever writes the database can hold a lock for good, or flood a
table the minute's run reads: the run then fails and the next tries again,
where it would have hung with its findings inside. A minute's read that
fails does not stop the signing: the run reads what waits alone, and fails
after it has moved it. The next minute's run tries the check again.

The signer cannot say of itself that it is stopped. It keeps when its last
minute's check completed, and whoever runs it alerts once that is minutes
old: its runs fail, or nothing runs it. A check completes once the minute's
upkeep after it has too, the pruning, the deletions and the re-check: one a
writer makes fail, with a lock on `log_tiles` or on a lapsed row, or a row
too large to read, stops the signer as a failed check does, rather than
leave the re-check off unsaid. What the upkeep learns is kept before the
next of its steps can fail. The deletions' entry is expected from the moment
its change commits, so a writer who then fails the plain statement after it
cannot drop the entry and put the rows back unseen: `missing`. The re-check
comes last, so what it finds is stored in the step that ends the minute, with
nothing between to fail.

**What runs it** is the object's alarm (`packages/vault/src/signer-host.ts`,
and `cloudflare-signer.ts` around it). One alarm is always set, for the
next of two things: a second after the vault tells of an entry, a run; a
minute after the last check, a run that checks. More entries than a run
takes, and the next run comes at once. A run that fails is tried again a
second later for as long as it takes, and a check that fails a minute
later. A signer nobody tells of anything checks all the same, from the
first time anyone asks how it stands.

The vault Worker exports the class and binds it, in `vault/wrangler.jsonc`:

```jsonc
"durable_objects": { "bindings": [{ "name": "SIGNER", "class_name": "CoffreSigner" }] },
"migrations": [{ "tag": "signer", "new_sqlite_classes": ["CoffreSigner"] }]
```

Its storage is the object's SQLite-backed key-value store. A step's changes
are one transaction, however many keys they touch, and on disk before the
step is done. A signer whose storage refused a write is dropped, and the
next run opens another from the storage. It has one database client, which
opens with a run's first statement and closes after 5 seconds idle.

**Alerts** go to the function the vault's configuration names:

```ts
alert: ({ text }) => fetch(env.SLACK_WEBHOOK, { method: 'POST', body: JSON.stringify({ text }) }).then(() => undefined),
```

It is called after a run with what the run found: `text`, ready to post,
which names the first ten findings and counts the rest, and `findings`, all
of them. Without it, alerts go to the Worker's log. The alarm for the next
run is set before it is called, and it has 5 seconds: one that has not
returned by then has failed. So a webhook that hangs holds the next run
back by 5 seconds, and costs none, nor does an object stopped in the
middle of an alert.

- A finding is let go once the function has returned. One that throws is
  called again after the next run with the same findings: twice is better
  than never.
- A row that is wrong, or a tree, is a finding when a check first finds it
  so, and again only if a check found it right in between: the signer
  keeps what its last check found wrong. Example: Bo's grant is put back
  at 12:00:30. The 12:01 check alerts. The checks at 12:02 and after say
  nothing, while the Settings page shows it standing. An Owner deletes the
  row at 12:10, and the writer puts it back at 12:20: the 12:21 check
  alerts again, whether or not an alert failed in between. A row found
  wrong whose alert failed is still said once, even if the next check
  finds it right, and waits as one finding however long alerts fail, and
  however often it is put right and wrong again meanwhile.
- A check names 1,000 rows at most, the first by name, the same at each
  check. After a restore from a backup every row changed since is wrong,
  and a writer can make as many wrong as they like: the alert still names
  rows, and the rest come with the checks after those are put right. With
  more than 1,000 wrong, one that a newly wrong row pushes past the
  thousandth is said a second time when it comes back among them.
- Once no check has completed for 5 minutes, the alert is `stopped`, with
  when the last one did. Said once, until a check completes, and again the
  next time it stops, whether alerts went through in between or not. A
  stop that ends before any alert went through is not said: its findings
  are, and the check that ended it has read every row and both trees. That
  covers runs that fail, on the database or on a lock its writer holds,
  and entries made to wait so that no check can run. It does not cover an
  object that never runs, nor one whose own storage refuses every write:
  then the Settings page shows the last check getting old, and for the
  first the next wake-up sets the alarm again.
- If alerts cannot be sent until more than 1,000 findings wait, they are
  written to the Worker's log and let go. The signer keeps them in one
  value of its storage, which must stay small. It then forgets what its
  last check found wrong, so a row or a tree still wrong is a finding
  again at the next check, and said once alerts go again. Until that check
  the Settings page shows nothing standing. An entry removed is said in
  the log only.

**The order of entries** is their id in `log_pending`, which for changes is
the order they committed in: each takes its id under the change lock, held
until its commit shows. Uses commit in any order and write no row.

**The window slides with the signer, not the clock.** The signer takes an
entry dated no more than 60 seconds before its last read that took
everything waiting, and remembers what it signed for as long as it would
still take it. So its memory is bounded, and an entry put back later is
refused either way: remembered, or too old. A fixed window by the clock was
the first design, and it would have lost entries: the signer is away for an
hour, say during an outage of its storage, and every entry written in that
hour is "too old" when it returns. With the sliding window it takes them all.
The window must outlast the longest a commit or a wake-up takes.

More than 256 entries waiting are taken over several runs, one after the
other. Until a read has taken everything, the signer does not say an expected
entry is missing, does not compare the rows (a row whose entry still waits
would be a false alert) and does not move its window. The minute's check
stays owed until a run finds nothing left waiting.

**What the minute's run checks of the log** is that each tree ends with the
entry signed last, at its leaf. That is one step down an index whatever the
log's size, where a count would read all of it. So a log cut back or
replaced at its end is found within the minute, if nothing was signed since.
An entry deleted or replaced further back is not, nor the last one once
another has been signed after it: the re-check of old entries finds those,
or a full verify (D222).

Example: entries 0 to 5 are signed. The writer truncates the log to entry 3.
The minute's run finds the log ending at 3, not 5: `cut`. Had the writer
deleted only entry 2, the log would still end at 5, and only the re-check
would find it.

**The re-check of old entries** (D222, `packages/vault/src/upkeep.ts`)
reads three blocks a minute, after the check: a block is the 256 entries of
one lowest-level tile. It walks the tree of changes, then the uses from the
first one kept, each as far as it was when the walk started, and round
again. A walk starts a day after the last one started at the soonest, so
that a small log is not read again every minute, nor a finding in it said
again every minute. Its end is fixed at its start, in the signer's storage
with where it is: a log growing faster than 768 entries a minute would
otherwise keep the walk in the uses for good, and never bring it back to
the changes.

- **The slice.** 3 blocks, 768 entries, read by index in one statement with
  the tiles that prove them, whatever the log's size. A log of a million
  entries is 3,907 blocks, walked in 1,303 minutes, about 22 hours. Past
  1.1 million a walk takes longer than a day and starts again at once: two
  million take 43 hours.
- **What it holds a block to.** The block's tile as stored must prove
  against the signer's own root, through the tiles above it as stored, and
  each entry must hash to its place in that tile. An entry that does not, or
  is not there, is `lost`, by the hash it was signed under. A block whose
  tiles do not prove, or with none of its entries there, is `lost` by its
  leaves, `aging 512-767`: one finding, not 256.
- **Said once a walk.** A block that stays wrong is said again at the next
  walk, so a deletion is said about once a day until a backup is restored.
- **What was pruned** is the signer's own record, not the database's: the
  walk starts the uses at the first one it kept. A use deleted below that
  point is pruned already, and nothing to find. One deleted above it is
  `lost`, whether or not it is past the keep.

Example: the writer deletes use 800 of a log of 1,280 changes and 1,034 uses.
The next walk reads changes 0 to 767, then 768 to 1,279 and the uses' block 0,
then the uses' blocks 1 to 3, and says `lost` with use 800's hash in its
third minute.

A writer who keeps more than 256 entries waiting holds the minute's check
off, since it runs only once nothing waits. Forged ones are each reported.
Genuine ones made to wait, by a trigger that slows the move say, are not:
what tells is the time of the last check, which stops moving on.

| Finding | What the signer saw |
|---|---|
| `forged` | A waiting entry whose MAC is not the vault's. It is removed |
| `replayed` | An entry it remembers signing, or its twin in the same read, put back. Removed |
| `stale` | An entry dated before its window, which nothing remembers signing. Removed |
| `missing` | An entry the vault told it of, gone before it was signed |
| `rewritten` | A change that replaced a row, or was decided on one, whose MAC is not the one the mirror holds: false history |
| `row` | On the minute's run, a row that is not what the entries say: changed, put back, deleted, or written behind the vault |
| `cut` | On the minute's run, a tree that does not end with the entry signed last |
| `lost` | An entry signed that is not in the log as signed: once moved, gone or changed where it waited, or another row in its place; or later, by the re-check, edited or deleted, or a block whose tiles do not prove |
| `stopped` | The host's own: no check has completed for 5 minutes |

**The vault's wake-up** goes through its `SIGNER` binding, once a use's or a
change's write has committed. The reply does not wait for it: the call
lives on until the signer has heard (`ctx.waitUntil`). A wake-up that fails
is written to the Worker's log and costs the caller nothing: the entry is
signed by the signer's next run, a minute's check at the latest, or for an
object that has never run, at the next wake-up that reaches it. What is
lost is the signer's word to expect the entry, the gap
[above](#what-it-promises). A vault Worker with
no `SIGNER` binding answers no call, and says why: its entries would wait
unsigned with nothing to tell.

*Not built yet.* The deployments' side: the examples, the dev deployment,
`coffre init` and `coffre update` exporting `CoffreSigner`, binding `SIGNER`
with its migration, and giving the vault its `workloads`. The vault decides
on `workloads` (`noReads`, `useOnly`) now, so a deployment says it to the
vault as well as to the app, and the app is to check that the two agree
when it starts, through `about`. The vault on Node takes its wake-up from
whoever opens it, until the signer runs there. The Settings page's last check and what stands
(`status` on the object). The object's call of `adopt` (adopt.ts), for its
signer, at the vault's first call, then a run. Until then its alarm must not
run a check: on an upgraded database every row is unsealed, and the mirror
empty, so each would be `row`. The adoption's first run writes the whole
mirror, one key per row, in one transaction of the object's storage: 100,000
at that size.

One limit, settled with the storage's loss (see
[Still to design](#still-to-design)): a removed entry (`forged`,
`replayed`, `stale`) is named in its alert by its hash only and its body is
gone, which for a `stale` one, a genuine entry, loses what it said.

## The server

The app (`@coffre/server`) keeps what the vault does not do: reading a
request, the pages' queries, sign-in with a provider, the proxy's work on a
request. It holds no key, and decides no access to a value or to a change.

**A request's path.**

1. *The credential.* A bearer token in `Authorization`, or the session's
   cookie. A change sent with a cookie must come from one of coffre's own
   pages (same origin), as in 0.14. A handle is turned away here: it is the
   proxy's.
2. *What it showed.* The app hashes the token (SHA-256) and puts it with
   the request's id and address. That is `Presented`, which every call to
   the vault starts with. The app verifies nothing about the token and
   looks nothing up to decide anything: it has no table of sessions to
   read. (For a workload's request and the proxy's, it reads the
   credential's row to find a gate, a place and a tunnel's end, and for
   an exchange the service's bindings: see "What the app reads of a
   workload".)
3. *The route.* `METHOD /path` is matched against the route table, its path
   segments and its input are checked, and its handler runs.
4. *The handler* has one of three shapes:
   - a use: `POST /reveals` calls `vault.use` and words the answer;
   - a change: `PATCH /secrets/market/prod` calls `vault.change`;
   - a page read: `GET /me` asks `vault.caller`, then queries the app's
     tables, scoped by the answer.
5. *The answer.* A refusal is given its status and its words in one place
   (`refusal` in `api/errors.ts`). Whatever throws is a fault: 503 when the
   database or the vault does not answer, 500 for a bug.

| Request | Vault calls | App statements |
|---|---|---|
| A reveal, a write | 1 | 0 |
| A page read | 1, `caller`, shared by every call of one render | the page's own |
| An environment's version | 1, `peek` | 0 |
| A write's dry run | 1, `weigh`, and 1 `use` per 1,000 values it would replace, to compare them | 0 |

Each route's own budget is in `packages/server/test/request-cost.test.ts`,
which also holds it to the same, its statements and the rows of a caller's
own view, however the instance grows, and fails on a route the API's table
serves with no budget there (one not served yet is `unserved.ts`'s).

In 0.14 every request ran one statement and one vault call before its
route (find the credential, ask `access`), resolved its path in 1 to 5
statements, checked the route's permissions, and logged each refusal in a
transaction of its own. None of that is left. The app has no credential
lookup, no permission check before a use or a change, no log to write, and
no answer of the vault's kept between requests.

Example: Bo runs `coffre set market/prod/API_KEY`. The app reads his bearer
token, hashes it, matches `PATCH /secrets/:project/:environment`, checks
the body, and calls `change({ op: 'secret.write', token, project: 'market',
environment: 'prod', values: [...] })`. The vault answers
`{ answer: { versions: { API_KEY: 3 } } }`, or `{ refused: 'no_access' }`,
which the app words as a 403. The app ran no statement.

**What a refusal becomes.**

| The vault says | The API answers |
|---|---|
| `no_grants` | 400 `bad_request` |
| `unauthenticated` | 401 `unauthenticated` |
| `bulk_limit` | 403 `bulk_limit` |
| `sensitive`, `not_owner_issued`, `use_only`, `parent_unreadable`, `workload_cannot_issue`, `not_a_person` | 403 `forbidden` |
| `unknown_key`, `unknown_version`, `no_value`, `no_reference`, `not_a_member`, `no_folder`, `unknown_identity`, `unknown_code`, `unknown_credential`, `unknown_principal` | 404 `not_found` |
| `unreadable`, `broken_chain`, `exists`, `referenced`, `reference_to_itself`, `reference_to_reference`, `not_inherited`, `inherited`, `parent_archived`, `too_deep`, `removed`, `last_owner`, `configured_owner`, `invalid` | 409 `conflict` |
| `adopting` | 503 `adopting`, with `Retry-After: 5` |
| `tampered` | 500 `tampered`, said once in the server's log |
| any other, `no_access` first among them | 403 `vault_refused` |

Every one carries the vault's word as `reason`, and the codes are the ones
0.14 answered for the same cases. An area adds its operations' refusals to
that table as it is served.

`tampered` is a fault, not the caller's: a row the vault would decide on
is not one it wrote. Its message names no row and no table, the same on
every route. The server logs it once, `console.error`, with the route's
pattern (`POST /device-logins/:code`) and the request's id, never anything
the caller typed (`noteTampered` in `api/errors.ts`): whoever runs coffre
must look. `adopting` is the vault before it has taken over a database
upgraded from 0.14, which a moment's wait mends, as the app's other 503s.

One difference from 0.14 follows from the vault's single `no_access`: a
place that does not exist and one the caller holds nothing in are both a
403, where 0.14 answered 404 for the first.

**Projects and environments** (`api/routes/places.ts`) are the first area
plugged in. `GET /projects` is one `caller` and two statements: the places
the caller reaches, with their folders, then the keys of those they may
open, counted by one `GROUP BY` (a fork's through its chain, as a use
resolves it), however many projects, environments and keys there are.
`GET /personal/:project` is one `caller` and one statement. Every change
is one `change` and no statement, and answers the place as the vault left
it. Where 0.14 answered otherwise:

- A `PATCH` makes one change: a new slug, archived or restored, the folder,
  or the protection. 0.14 took several in one body, in one transaction; the
  vault makes each one alone, so two would leave the first made when the
  second is refused, and the API answers 400 instead. A slug the place has
  already is no change, so the settings page's `{ slug, folder }` passes.
- `PUT` of a place that is there is refused, 409 `exists`, where 0.14
  answered it unchanged, `created: false`. The vault refuses it to whoever
  could make it, and the app holds no row to answer with.
- A folder renamed or emptied (`PATCH` and `DELETE /folders/:folder`) is one
  change of the vault's, `folder.refile`: every project filed there, by
  whoever manages each one, or none, as 0.14's one transaction did. A
  folder none of whose projects the caller may file is `no_access`, where
  0.14 answered 404, or 403 for one they saw part of.
- An environment added to an archived project, an archived or deleted
  parent, and taking a protection off without owning the project are each
  `no_access`, 403, where 0.14 answered 409 `project_archived`, 404 and 403
  `forbidden`.
- `name` is taken and changes nothing: a place has gone by its slug since
  0.6.2, and every answer gives the slug as its name.
- A fork that copies, or holds references (`from` with no `inherit`), is
  one change, `environment.create` with `copy` (`EnvironmentCopy` in
  @coffre/core): the environment and every key it holds, all of them or
  none. 0.14 made the environment first, then logged a `secret.read` of
  each value with the purpose `copy`, handed the values to the app and wrote
  them back as `secret.write`s, and filed the folders in a third
  transaction; a fork that stopped midway left the environment, empty or
  half filled, and the same `PUT` again filled one with no live key. Now no
  value leaves the vault, so nothing is read and the one entry says each
  key made, copied from which secret or referring to it; a `PUT` of an
  environment that is there is `exists`, as any. Its keys are resolved as a
  use resolves them: a key that 0.14 forbade, 403, because it comes from a
  parent the forker cannot read is `parent_unreadable`, still 403, and a
  sensitive value reached through a reference whose source they could not
  refer to, 0.14's 403, is `no_access`. A copied value's first date
  (`first_written_at`) is its source's, as a restore's is: the member
  report does not count its forker as having written it, who never saw it,
  and counts whoever reads the copy as knowing it. Who read the source
  only is not counted as knowing the copy, a key of its own.
- A place deleted for good (`DELETE /projects/:project[/:environment]`,
  `?dryRun=1` to preview, which is `weigh`) is one change. Whoever does not
  run the instance is refused `no_access`, 403, where 0.14 answered 403
  `forbidden`. 0.14's 409s for a place restored, granted or referred to
  while it was being deleted are gone: the change starts over on rows that
  moved, and answers as they are then. A personal config is named by its
  slug, as the list of them shows, or `dev@me`; 0.14 logged one entry for
  it and one for the deletion, and the one entry here names both. A place
  past 20,000 versions holding a value is `too_large`, 409, which 0.14
  erased in one transaction however large.

**Writing keys** (`api/routes/secrets.ts`): a value, one a client
generated by a recipe and the version it replaces, a reference, an
archive (`null`) in `PATCH /secrets/:project/:environment`; a key brought
back or filed in `PATCH …/:key` (`archived`, `folder`), or reset to what
a fork inherits (`inherit`); a version restored
in `POST …/:key/restore`, answered with the version it is now and its
sensitivity, where 0.14 gave the version only; a reference broken
in `DELETE …/:key/reference`; a folder of keys renamed or emptied in
`PATCH` and `DELETE /folders/:project/:environment/:folder`. Each is one
change of the vault's and no statement of the app. `PATCH …/:key` answers
the key and what was asked of it, which is what it now is: 0.14 also
answered what was not asked, and a reset with the parent it reads. Each
is served in `dev@me`, the caller's personal config of dev, which a write
of a value makes where they have none; a reference there is a 400, since
a config holds values only. A rename (`key`) is a change of its own,
`secret.rename`: asked beside an archiving or a folder, which 0.14 made in
one transaction, it is a 400.

**An environment's keys** (`api/routes/keys.ts`): its list, a key's
versions, the references into and out of a place, what an environment is
missing, and its version. The first four are each one `caller` and one
statement, which reads the place it names: the list, the environment and
the environments it inherits from with their keys; a key's history, one
page of its versions; the references, those of one project (and, for an
access manager, one more for their readers, below); the missing keys, its
project's key names. None reads a value but a plain one's
`plaintext` copy, and none asks the vault anything past who the caller
is. `dev@me` is the caller's personal config of dev, or dev while they
have none, as in 0.14.

The list shows a plain value as 0.14 did: its own, a live reference's
source's, and an inherited one to a lister of the parent that holds it.
It comes from the `plaintext` copy of the current version, and only
while the secret's row says the key is plain: a secret whose version
carries a copy shows none. `readable` is 0.14's: a value not sensitive,
and plain or where the caller holds `secret.read` (for an inherited key,
at the parent that holds it too). It is worked out from the caller's
permissions and the row alone. The vault decides again at the reveal.
`rotateAfterDays` is the deployment's setting.

The environment's version (`GET /projects/:project/:environment/version`,
which `coffre export --watch` asks before it reads) is one `peek` of the
vault's and no statement of the app's, where it was one `caller` and one
statement. `peek` reads and decides as a reveal of the whole environment
would, from rows whose MACs it checked and the vault's memory, and
answers each key's secret and version, or why it is refused. It opens
nothing and writes nothing: no log entry, no exposure, no credential's
last use. A reveal within the vault's memory after it reads nothing
again. The app hashes the answer with the reveal's own `versionOf`, and
refuses as the reveal would (`stopOf`, beside the reveal in
`api/routes/secrets.ts`). Where 0.14 answered otherwise:

  older ones and `more` to say there are. 0.14 answered all of them. How a
  value was made is kept for the current version alone, on its secret's row.
- A reference is a column of its holder's row, `source_id`, with who
  pointed it there and when (`source_set_by`, `source_set_at`), which are
  its `createdBy` and `createdAt`. An ended one is no reference: its row
  keeps no source, and nothing says who ended it, so `endedBy` and
  `endedAt` are null; the log has it (`reference.break`). Who reads

  either project's access, and is null to anyone else: the active members

  it, the roles they hold as their rows define them (`readersAt`, in
  `api/routes/members.ts`). It costs that page one statement more, however
  many references it shows, and none when it shows a manager none. It is
  for display: it decides nothing, and no credential's scope narrows it.
  A key named in `GET /references` that holds none is an empty list,

  end names it; 0.14 answered such a place an empty list, and one not
  there 404, to anyone.

**The member report** (`GET /members/:member`), 0.14.7's offboarding
report, is for whoever runs the instance, removed members included; anyone
else is refused `no_access` before any statement. It is one `caller` and
one statement (`reportOf`): the member's row; what still lets them in at
their generation, counted (live grants, sessions, tokens, connected apps,
sign-in accounts); their personal configs; and the live keys whose current
value they know (`exposed`), each `read` or `wrote`, with the date that
makes it so (`at`). Keys they knew a value of that has since changed are
only counted (`rotated`). A key is theirs to rotate when they last saw it
(`exposures.seen_at`) on or after the date its current value was first
written (`first_written_at`), or when they wrote that value: a version of
theirs that is no restore (`created_at = first_written_at`) first wrote it.
Example: Dee writes `STRIPE_KEY` v1, Al writes v2, then restores v1 as v3.
v3 is Dee's to rotate, `wrote`, and not Al's: he restored a value he never
knew. A read through a reference counts against its source. A key
archived, or in an archived or deleted environment or project, is left out
until it comes back. The statement starts from the member's own rows
(`exposures` by principal, `secret_versions_created_by_idx`), and joins
their keys by primary key once the instance holds many more keys than they
touched. The directory's `toRotate` counts the same for every removed
member, in one pass over their rows inside its one statement. The same
statement lists the service tokens the member issued that still work
(`issuedTokens`): of a service account still there, at its generation, not
revoked or expired, by account and oldest first, whatever became of the
member, since removing them ends none. It finds them by account, one probe
of `credentials_issued_by_idx` (principal, created_by) for each service
account: 1.6 ms for 500 accounts and 200,000 credentials
(drive: `W419-plans/member-report-lists-explain.txt`). A person's own live
personal tokens and handles (`personalTokens`), newest first, are what
`live.tokens` counts and what their tokens page lists, each scope by path
as there; a service account's are on its page, so its list is empty. A
person's connected apps (`apps`), newest first, are what `live.apps`
counts and what Connected apps lists (`connectedApps`): the connections
their clients redeemed, 20 at most.
The references they made that still stand (`references`), oldest first,
then live ones first, each as the keys' lists show it: a key whose row
points at a source and says they pointed it (`source_set_by`), held in a key,
an environment and a project neither archived nor deleted. An ended one
lends nothing and is not listed, where 0.14.7 listed it as `replaced` or
with who ended it: the log keeps it (`reference.break`, or the write over
it). Their references are found among every reference the instance holds,
by `secrets_source_idx`, with no index of their own: 3.6 ms for 20 of
20,000 at 200,000 keys, 7.5 ms for 1,000 of 21,000; an index on the maker
would read 0.7 ms and 5.2 ms (drive: `W419-plans/member-references-explain.txt`).
Each of the three lists of tokens and references stops at 200, and
`totals` says how many there are in all: nothing caps a principal's live
tokens, or what they made. Where 0.14.7 answered otherwise: `declared` is
false, as in the directory.

**Freezes** (`api/routes/freezes.ts`, [workload access](workload-access.md),

**The instance's roles and settings** (`api/routes/roles.ts`,

and one statement (`db/queries/policy.ts`): every role's row, with the
grants that name each project role and the active people who hold each
instance role, counted by role. `GET /settings`, for whoever runs the
instance, is one `caller` and one statement: the policy row, with the
slugs of the projects its scope names. A change of a role
(`PUT`/`DELETE /roles/:role`, `/instance-roles/:role`) is one
`policy.change`, and a change of the settings (`PUT /settings`) one

weighed first, as 0.14.7 weighed it (`?dryRun=1`): `caller` and three

naming it is on; for an instance role, every one), from which the app

grants of one member, or environments; past any of them, the preview is

gathering is all; they are sorted once taken). The preview answers the role's `revision`
(`roleRevision`, a digest of its row, or of none), which the change is
confirmed with (`?revision=`): the vault refuses it `policy_changed`
unless the role's row still says what was weighed, and makes a change
asked without one from the role as it is. Where 0.14 answered otherwise:

- A revision is the role's, not the policy's: 0.14.7's covered every
  role and protected environment, so an unrelated change, a rename of a
  protected environment included, made a confirmed edit weigh again. The
  preview does not ask whether a grant on an environment would refuse a
  widening; the change says so, 409 `invalid`.
- Defining a role that does not ship, where no row is, is refused 409
  `role_granted` while a grant names it, as deleting it is: the grant was
  kept from its deletion, and would come back to life.
- A role's change answers the role as the vault now holds it, with
  `grants` null: it reads no row to count them. `GET /roles` counts every
  grant row that names a role, of a membership before a removal included,

- Whoever may not edit roles, or read or change the settings, is refused
  `no_access`, where 0.14 said `forbidden`; both are 403. A change the

**Behind Cloudflare Access** (`behind-access.ts`). Access is a sign-in
provider like GitHub, whose sign-in happens before the request arrives:
Access sends its signed assertion with every request, and the vault finds
a caller only by a credential's hash. So a browser holds a session of
coffre's, which its first request makes. Every request but `/livez`,
`/readyz` and `GET /api/auth` is checked first, in `respond`, once, and
the page's client and coffre's route use the session the check admits:

1. The assertion is verified: the team's keys, RS256 or ES256, issuer,
   audience, expiry, no clock skew. With none, or one that fails, the
   request has no session, whatever cookie it carries: Access's own
   revocation shuts the door on the next request. A malformed one, or one
   under another algorithm, is refused before any key is fetched.
2. The session cookie counts when the companion cookie,
   `__Host-coffre_access`, sealed under a key derived from the app key,
   says the session was signed in for this assertion's subject and this
   token. Nothing more is asked: the request costs what it costs with
   coffre's own sign-in.
3. Otherwise (no session, someone else's assertion on a shared browser, a
   companion that does not open) the person signs in: one `signin` change,
   `{ provider: 'cloudflare-access', issuer: sha256hex(<team origin>),
   subject: <sub>, emails: [<email>] }`, which ends the session the browser
   showed. Both cookies are set on the response, for
   `cloudflareAccess({ browserSessionHours })`, 12 hours unless set.

Only a browser signs in: a request with Access's own cookie,
`CF_Authorization`, and nothing in `Authorization`. The CLI through
`cloudflared`, an Access service token (`common_name`, no subject), a bearer
token and a workload's session (`Authorization: DPoP`) get `401
unauthenticated` saying that behind Access only browsers sign in, before any
row is read or a workload's gate asked: signing them in would make a session
row a command. A session the vault no
longer finds (ended elsewhere, expired, its member removed) answers 401 and
clears both cookies, so the next request signs in again. Someone Access
vouches for who is no member, or a removed one, is refused `not_registered`
or `deactivated`: `GET /me` answers who they are with `registered: false`,
which their pages say, and every other route 403 `registration_required`;
each of their requests asks `signin` again, which writes nothing. Signing
out ends coffre's session, clears both cookies, and sends the browser on to
`/cdn-cgi/access/logout`, without which the next request would sign in
again; a sign-out on the request that signed in (no cookies, or a companion
that does not open) ends that new session and sets only the clearing
cookies. The team's keys are fetched through the app's transport, kept ten
minutes, and fetched again for a key they lack at most every 30 seconds,
counted from the last refresh started, landed or not: of a burst naming a
new key, one request fetches and the others are refused. The verifier
keeps settled values only, never a fetch in flight. When
they cannot be fetched, the request answers 503 `unavailable`, said once in
the server's log.

| Request behind Access | Vault calls | App statements |
|---|---|---|
| With a session and its companion | as with coffre's own sign-in | as with coffre's own sign-in |
| A browser's first, a new person on it, or a companion that does not open | `signin`, then the request's own | the request's own |
| No assertion, one that fails, the CLI, a service token, a bearer | none (401) | 0 |
| Someone who is no member | `signin`, refused (`GET /me` answers, others 403) | 0 |

  entry, and answered a service account two empty lists. Behind Cloudflare
  Access both are 404, as in 0.14.7. 0.14.7 listed every row; a person
  signing in from a script can hold thousands of live sessions, so the
  sessions list stops at the 200 last used and the accounts list at the
  50 oldest (sign-in itself bounds neither), each reading one row past
  its cap to answer `more`.

**MCP's OAuth from the server** (`mcp/http.ts`, `mcp/oauth.ts`,
`api/routes/oauth.ts`, [MCP connections](#operations)). coffre is the
authorization server of the MCP clients a person connects. The app reads
the authorization request, fetches a client's metadata document, draws the
code and the tokens, and counts the requests strangers make; each write is
the vault's, in the one change a request makes:

| Path | Vault call | App statements | What the app does |
|---|---|---|---|
| `GET /.well-known/oauth-protected-resource[/mcp]`, `GET /.well-known/oauth-authorization-server` | none | 0 | the metadata (RFC 9728, RFC 8414) |
| `GET /oauth/authorizations` (the consent page's read) | `caller` | 1, the client's registration and the person's connections of it | the request checked; a metadata document fetched unless kept; refused to another site's page first |
| `POST /oauth/authorizations`, Approve | `caller`, then `mcp.connect` | 1 for a registered client, its row; 0 for a document's | the request checked again; the code drawn; the redirect with the code, the state and the issuer (RFC 9207) |
| `POST /oauth/authorizations`, Deny | `caller` | as Approve | the redirect with `access_denied`; nothing written |
| `POST /api/oauth/token`, a code or a refresh token | `mcp.token` | 0 | the access and refresh tokens drawn; the vault's refusals worded as RFC 6749's; `ended` answered `invalid_grant` |
| `POST /api/oauth/register` | `oauth.register` | 0 | public clients only; the redirects coffre takes kept, the others left out |
| `POST /api/oauth/revoke` | `mcp.revoke`, or none for a string no MCP token's shape | 0 | 200 whatever the token |
| `GET /apps` | `caller` | 1 | the caller's connections their clients redeemed |
| `DELETE /apps/:id` | `credential.end`, of the caller's or anyone's | 0 | |

The token, registration and revocation endpoints, which anyone may call,
pass the deployment's limits first, per source and in total, before the
body is read; so does the consent page before it fetches a document.
`request-cost.test.ts` holds each path to its row. Text the vault takes
none of (a `client_id` past 256 characters, a NUL or half a surrogate pair
in it, in a redirect or in a name) never reaches it: such a client or
redirect is `invalid_grant` at the token endpoint and asks nothing at the
revocation one; a registration leaves such a redirect out, as any other it
does not take, and drops such characters from the name. On the consent
page such a `client_id` is an invalid request, said before any statement:
Postgres takes no NUL in a query's parameter either. A name is cut to the
100 characters the vault keeps, the host's too when a client's document
gives none: `https://` and a host of 119 characters is a valid client, whose
host the page shows cut.

Example: Claude sends Ada to `/oauth/authorize?client_id=https://claude.ai/…`.
The page's render asks `GET /oauth/authorizations` on her session: the
vault says who she is, one statement reads her connections of Claude, the
app fetches Claude's document and checks the redirect is one it lists. She
ticks Read and Reveal and approves: the app checks the request again (the
document is kept ten minutes), draws a code and asks `mcp.connect` with
its SHA-256 and Claude's PKCE challenge, then sends the browser to
Claude's callback with the code. Claude posts the code and its verifier to
`/api/oauth/token`; the app draws `coffre_mcp_…` and `coffre_mcr_…` and
asks `mcp.token` with their hashes; the vault checks the verifier itself.

What the app reads for the consent page and Connected apps is unchecked,
as every page's: a registration's row, the person's connections. Each
decision is the vault's own read. A row written behind the vault can show
a page something, and change nothing:

- A registration's row edited shows its name and redirects on the consent
  page, and a request it refuses (a wrong `response_type`, say) is sent to
  an edited redirect with its error; Approve is refused `tampered` (500),
  as the vault reads the row under its MAC. A row hidden shows the client
  as unknown.
- A connection's row edited or hidden changes what Connected apps lists,
  and what the consent page says a step-up ends; ending one is the vault's
  `credential.end`, on its own read.

Where 0.14.7 answered otherwise:

- Deny writes nothing: 0.14.7 logged `mcp.connect` denied
  (`person_denied`). Nothing changed, and the vault logs its changes.
- Approve and the consent page's read are a person's on a session of
  theirs (`no_access` for any token); 0.14.7 showed the page to any person.
- A registration past the table's ceiling, 10,000, is 503
  `temporarily_unavailable` (`full`); 0.14.7 had no ceiling.
- Revocation of a string that is no MCP token's shape asks the vault
  nothing; of the refresh token before the one replaced last, ends nothing,
  as `mcp.token` holds it.
- A client's name is cut to 100 characters, then trimmed, on a whole
  character: the name the vault keeps is the name as shown.

**`/mcp`** (`mcp/endpoint.ts`, `mcp/tools.ts`), the endpoint the access
tokens are for: JSON-RPC, one JSON answer a request, no stream and no
session, the 2026-07-28 revision and the 2025 era's `initialize` beside it,
as 0.14.7 spoke them. Each request is the vault's `caller` on the access
token's hash first, which says who the connection's person is, all they
hold now, the connection and its scopes; a bearer that is no access
token's shape (`coffre_mcp_…`) is 401 before the vault is asked, as is
any request with an `Origin` but coffre's own. A tool runs the API's own
handlers in process (`runRoute` in `api/router.ts`), on that answer and the
token's hash: what the route reads and asks the vault is what the tool
does, and the vault decides each use and change on the access token's
own rules (a value only with `reveal`, no change at all). 0.14.7 made a
second in-process request with an identity it forged; its table of the
scope each route needs through MCP (`mcp/scopes.ts`) goes with it: a tool
names its scope, and the vault holds a value to `reveal` whatever a tool
says. What the route throws, the endpoint maps as the API would: a refusal,
and the database or the vault not answering (the API's 503 `unavailable`),
is the tool's error, which the model reads and may try again; a row the
vault did not write, or a bug, is JSON-RPC's internal error, its details in
the server log alone.

| Request | Vault calls | App statements |
|---|---|---|
| `initialize`, `ping`, `server/discover`, `tools/list` | `caller` | 0 |
| `tools/call`, a read tool (`list_secrets`, say) | `caller` | the route's (1 for `list_secrets`), on the same answer |
| `tools/call`, `reveal_secret_values` | `caller`, then `use` | 0 |

The tools are 0.14.7's Read tools on the routes 0.16 serves: `whoami`,
`list_projects`, `list_secrets`, `secret_history`, `list_access`,
`describe_member`, and `reveal_secret_values` with Reveal values; and,
through an approval (below), `show_secret_value` and
`request_secret_value`. `read_audit_log` comes back with `GET /audit`, and
0.14.7's other change tools on the approvals that are served now.
`tools/list` shows a person the tools their roles reach somewhere, as
0.14.7 did, from the `caller` answer.

Example: Claude calls `list_secrets` on `market/prod` with Ada's access
token. The vault's `caller` says it is Ada's, of her generation, under a
connection that stands, with `read`; the endpoint runs
`GET /secrets/:project/:environment`'s handler with that answer, and its one
statement lists the keys she may see. With `reveal_secret_values` the
handler's `use` asks the vault for the values on the same token's hash; the
vault checks the connection holds `reveal` (`mcp_no_reveal` otherwise) and
logs the read on the access token.

The limits: a connection's `tools/call`s pass the deployment's
`perConnection` limiter first, by the connection's id, as in 0.14.7 (429;
a limiter that fails refuses, and is logged); listing is no call. 0.14.7
counted nothing else at `/mcp`: its access token was a MAC it checked
reading nothing. Now a token the vault does not know costs a `caller`, one
indexed read on the token's hash, as any bearer on `/api` does, which no
limiter counts either; a string with no access token's shape costs
nothing.

Where 0.14.7 answered otherwise:

- No entry for each `tools/call` (0.14.7 wrote one in a transaction of its
  own): the vault's `use` and `change` log what matters, a reveal on the
  access token, whose connection is its one ancestor.
- A connection's end, its person's removal or freeze stop its access tokens
  within the two seconds the vault remembers a caller, as any credential's
  (0.14.7 read the connection's row at each request).
- An access token on a row the vault did not write is 401 `invalid_token`,
  as one the vault does not know.
- `whoami` names the connection by its id and scopes; 0.14.7 named its
  client, which only the connection's row holds.
- A reveal is `caller`, then `use`, where the plan had `use` alone: the
  connection's limit and the scope challenge need the connection and its
  scopes first.

**An MCP client's changes** (`mcp/approvals.ts`, `mcp/changes.ts`,
`routes/approvals.ts`; the vault's `mcp.ask`, `mcp.decide` and `via`), as
in 0.14.7 (docs/design/mcp.md, section 7): the client asks, its person
decides on coffre's own page, and the page makes the change. The vault
writes every step, as it writes a device login's: someone asks, a person
decides in the browser, and the asker polls.

Claude calls `request_secret_value` for `market/prod/STRIPE_KEY`. The
endpoint checks, as the connection, that Ada could write there (the
listing's one statement, a courtesy: the decision that counts is
Approve's, as Ada), then asks the vault for an approval on Claude's access
token (`mcp.ask`): a row of `mcp_approvals` with the tool, its arguments as
canonical JSON and their digest, the SHA-256 of `[tool, arguments]`,
pending for five minutes. Its entry, kept, names the tool, the place
(`market/prod/STRIPE_KEY`) and the digest, never the arguments: they are
the client's text, which only the row keeps. Claude opens `/approvals/<id>`
for Ada (URL elicitation), or shows her the link; nothing has changed.
Ada's browser reads the page (`GET /approvals/:id`): what the change is,
and what it replaces, read as her, on her session. She types the value and
approves: the page sends back the digest it rendered and the basis it
showed (a digest of what the change replaces: the key's current version by
its id, `versionId`, which no other key's version shares, the reference it
is and its source's id, or that it has neither). The route refuses, before asking the vault anything, an ask whose
row now holds other arguments (a database writer's edit: the digest of the
row's arguments is no longer the page's) or a key set again since.
Example: the page showed `API_KEY` at version 1; Bo renamed it `MOVED` and
made a new `API_KEY`, at version 1 too; its version's id is another, and
Approve is a 409. Then it makes the write `PATCH /secrets/:project/:environment`
makes, on her session, with `via` naming the connection, the approval and
the digest, and what it read as `replaces` (the version's id, `{ source }`
for a reference, `null` for a key with neither): one `change`, whose entry
is hers and names both.

The vault holds `via` (`ForConnection` in @coffre/core/vault,
`packages/vault/src/ops/via.ts`) for any change a person asks. It reads the
connection and the approval beside the change's own rows. The connection
must stand (redeemed, live, not frozen, with a write's time left), be of
the caller's own member and generation, and the caller be on a browser
session, or the change is refused `disconnected`. The approval must be
that connection's, pending, of the digest the page rendered, and unexpired
a write's time before its end, or the change is refused
`unknown_approval`. The write starts a write's time before either ends
(`Plan.until`, on the database's clock), or not at all: neither row changes
by lapsing, so reading them again under the lock would not see it. Nothing
else bounds it: Ada's session and grants may expire in that time and the
write is still made, as any change's is ("The caller's own time", above). Made, the change writes the approval approved in its
own write: no approval is approved without its change, and none is applied
twice. Of two Approves at once, or an Approve and a denial, the second to
write finds the approval's row changed under the change lock, starts over
and is refused. A refused change writes nothing but its entry, so a value
written between Approve's read and the change (`stale`), an expiry or a
disconnect leaves the approval pending: the page says why, and Ada reloads
it, sees the value now, and decides again. It decides nothing else: what
the change may do is Ada's. An MCP access token still makes no change
itself.

A denial is `mcp.decide`, one small change on Ada's browser session, as
`device.decide` is: the row written denied, its entry `mcp.deny`.
`show_secret_value` has no change to carry its approval, since `use` takes
no `via`. Approve reads the value first, with `POST /reveals` on Ada's
session, logged as her own reveal; then `mcp.decide` writes the approval
approved (`mcp.approve`), which it does only for a tool that changes
nothing (`MCP_SHOWS` in @coffre/core/mcp). Should that second step be
refused (the approval expired meanwhile, or was denied in another tab), Ada
still sees the value, hers to read, and the client hears the approval as
it stands: expired, or denied. The page alone shows the value; no row,
entry or answer to the client holds it, so a Read tool hands the model a
value only as `reveal_secret_values` does, with Reveal values. It needs
Read, as in 0.14.7.

The client hears how its approval ended by the approval's id, a read: a
client with URL elicitation echoes it as `requestState`; one without gets
the link and the id, and calls again with the same arguments and
`approval: "<id>"`, an argument every change tool takes, outside the
call's digest. One function reads the row for both: an unknown id, or
another connection's, is "no such approval"; one of this connection's for
other arguments, "not for this call"; one asked more than ten minutes ago,
expired, to ask anew. A named call writes nothing, however often it comes.
A call that names none joins the same call's pending approval (the vault
refuses the ask `pending`, and the app reads which), or opens a new one.
After a decision that is a new one, whose page says "You approved this
same request 2 minutes ago." when the same call of the same connection was
approved within ten minutes before it: what protects Ada from a model that
dropped the id.

What a client writes is bounded: a connection holds five approvals waiting
(`MAX_PENDING`), and only Ada's decision or their expiry frees one. A
decline from Claude's prompt writes nothing and frees nothing: the approval
waits on, and Ada may still decide it. A refused ask writes nothing, not
even its entry. So a client asking in a loop writes five `mcp.ask` entries
every five minutes at most, 1,440 a day, per connection, and Ada holds 20
connections at most.

`mcp_approvals` carries no MAC (D240): a row grants nothing, since the
change is Ada's, on her session, decided as hers. The vault's engine reads
and writes it as it reads a version (`Loose` in change.ts): read again
under the change lock by a hash of the whole row, and left out of the
entry's rows, which the signer holds to its mirror. The app's login only
reads it, as every other table. Its status is pending, approved or denied,
and what the client hears is said from that and `decided_at`: 0.16 writes
no `outcome`, and 0022 drops `reported_at`. The vault's upkeep deletes an
approval a day after its expiry, and a connection's end takes its own.

| Request | Vault calls | App statements |
|---|---|---|
| `tools/call`, a change asked | `caller`, `change` (`mcp.ask`) | the check's (1) |
| `tools/call`, the same call again, naming none, while it waits | `caller`, `change` (refused `pending`, nothing written) | the check's (1), the approval joined (1), its row (1 every second while it waits, 25 s at most) |
| `tools/call`, naming its approval | `caller` | its row (1, and 1 every second while it waits) |
| `GET /approvals/:id` | `caller` | 1, then what the preview reads as the person (1 for a secret's versions) |
| `POST /approvals/:id`, Approve | `caller`, then the route's own `change` (for a reveal, `use`, then `change`) | 1, what it replaces read again (1) |
| `POST /approvals/:id`, Deny | `caller`, `change` (`mcp.decide`) | 1 |

Where 0.14.7 answered otherwise:

- The ask is logged (`mcp.ask`, kept) as 0.14.7 logged `mcp.call`, by its
  tool, place and digest; a denial is `mcp.deny`, a reveal's approval
  `mcp.approve`, and a change's own entry names the connection and the
  approval (`via`, both among the objects). A view and a decline are
  logged nowhere.
- A change refused at Approve leaves its approval pending; 0.14.7 marked it
  failed and told the client why. The client waits on while Ada reloads.
- A decline leaves the approval pending until Ada decides it or it
  expires, its place held; 0.14.7 cancelled it, which freed the place.
- A client hears an approved change from its status alone:
  `request_secret_value`'s result is `{ secret }`, without the version
  number 0.14.7 gave, which a read tool still says.
- A client without `requestState` names its approval by the `approval`
  argument; 0.14.7's retry with the same arguments rejoined it, and marked
  it heard.
- A reveal on the page is logged as Ada's, on her session, as any reveal
  of hers, without the connection: `use` takes no `via`.
- The `requestState` a client echoes is the approval's id, not a sealed
  token: the row says which connection and which call it is for.
- Only `show_secret_value` and `request_secret_value` are served so far.

A reveal of one key that is refused refuses the request, with that key's
reason. A reveal of an environment answers the keys it was given, and its
version (a hash of each key with the version it resolved to, which
`coffre export --watch` compares). It names what it left out, as 0.14 did:
a person's sensitive values in `sensitive`, a workload's use-only ones in
`useOnly`. A key the environment does not hold, or one with no value, is
left out unnamed. Any other key that cannot be read refuses the whole read.

**Which routes a credential reaches.** 0.14 kept three tables beside the
routes: what a personal token, a workload's session and an MCP connection
may call. A token's and a session's are gone. The vault decides each use
and change on the credential's own scope, and a scoped credential holds
nothing of the instance's own (`runsInstance` in `@coffre/core/access`): a
personal token cannot add a member, whatever its person may do. An MCP
client's tokens (`coffre_mcp_…`, `coffre_mcr_…`) reach no route of the
API (`unauthenticated`), as in 0.14.7: an access token is good at `/mcp`,
and its scopes come back with it; a refresh token at the token endpoint.

**What is built: the skeleton.** 25 files, about 1,300 lines, in
`packages/server/src`: `app.ts` (health, the door below the migrations, the

`fetch-api.ts` (the same-origin rule, a page's client), `api/router.ts`,
`api/route.ts`, `api/context.ts`, `api/errors.ts`, `api/caller.ts` (what a
page may list, from the vault's answer), and three routes: `POST /reveals`
and `PATCH /secrets/:project/:environment` for values
(`api/routes/secrets.ts`), and `GET /me` (`api/routes/me.ts`), whose one
statement is in `db/queries/places.ts`.

It is held three ways. `packages/server/tsconfig.skeleton.json` typechecks
it alone, from its entry points, since 0.14's files beside it do not build.

that row is under.

The typed client reads the same table. `packages/client/src/api.ts` is the

each route's input and output as the JSON that crosses the wire, a route of

each a part of a route's input or output or a type a served route's file
exports. A type whose route is unserved has none until the route is served.

generate` in the same pull request: `packages/client/test/api.test.ts` fails
while `api.ts` differs from what the table says. A route's `run` with no
declared return type gets what TypeScript infers, such as `archivedAt: null`
where the route can answer a date: declare the output on the route, never in
`api.ts`.

### Node in 0.16.0

0.16.0 ships for Cloudflare Workers only, and Node comes back in 0.16.1
(D198). The signer on Node is [still to design](#still-to-design), and
`packages/server/src/node.ts` is 0.14's: it imports what 0.16 deleted

`examples/node` itself (`coffre init --node` writes it), `pnpm
conformance:node` and `pnpm bench:node` run by hand, and the Action's smoke
test, whose fixture boots `examples/node` for the CLI to talk to. That test
holds the CLI on Node 20 to 24, which ships in 0.16.0, so it is not skipped:
its fixture moves to the Workers example once that boots.

## Pages

A request asks the vault once who its caller is and what they hold. The app
scopes its queries with the answer.

Every page's shell reads `GET /me`, the caller's places by name: one
statement. Only the Projects page reads `GET /projects`, whose count of
keys reads every key of every environment the caller may open. 0.14 read
it from every page's shell.

| Page | Queries |
|---|---|
| Projects | The projects the caller holds, with their environment counts |
| Project | Its environments, with key counts |
| Environment | Its keys with current version, sensitivity and last use, no values but plain ones' copies; its version, one `peek` of the vault's |
| Key | Its last 20 versions, and its last 10 log entries through `log_index` |
| Proxy tab | The environment's `proxy_routes` row |
| A project's users and service accounts | Grants joined with principals, at the principal's generation |
| Users, service accounts | Principals, paged |
| One user or service account | Principal, identities, grants, tokens and bindings, activity through `log_index` |
| Settings | `policy`, `roles`, `log_head` and the last check's result |
| Audit | The newest 50 of `log`, or of `log_index` for one filter, and the tiles that prove them |
| Account | Own identities, passkeys, sessions and connected apps |
| Device login, OAuth consent, MCP approval | The one row being decided |

An audit page checks each entry it shows against the tree, under the head
the vault vouches for in the page's own `caller` call, so an entry edited
under that head is caught when someone looks at it, and one past it shows as
not proven.

`GET /audit` serves it (`packages/server/src/api/audit.ts`), in 0.14.7's
shapes with four additions: each entry's `proof`, the page's `head`, its
`holes` and `next`. One call to the vault, `caller` with `head`, then at most
three statements: the places the page is about (none for whoever reads the
whole log, with no path), a stretch of the log, and its tiles and names.

- **Proof.** `proven`: its body hashes to the leaf the head's tree holds at
  its place. `unproven`: past the head in both trees (its leaf past its own
  tree's size, and its seq less its leaf past the other's), or no head
  stands. `failed`: within the head and not what it signed, the body edited
  or a tile on the way up gone; or past one tree and within the other, a
  place no entry the head numbered holds: an entry's tree and leaf are
  columns a writer edits, and one moved there to dodge its proof fails.
  Within the head nothing else counts as proven.
- **A bounded stretch.** A page reads at most twice `limit` entries, a
  thousand at most, newest first: from `log` by its key, or, for a path, an
  actor or a reader who does not read the whole log, from `log_index`. There
  it takes the newest of each object it reads by, merged under a `LIMIT` of
  twice the stretch, which stops the merge, and only then counts an entry
  two of them name once: a project and its environment, which every entry
  about the environment names, fill the stretch together. What it returns
  is bounded whatever the plan; what it reads is the plan Postgres picks.
  Where the objects hold more than the merge takes, it streams each one's
  newest from its index and stops with the merge: twice the stretch in
  index rows and one more per object, however long the log (200 objects of
  300 entries each, a stretch of 1,000: 2,199 rows). Where its statistics
  say they hold about what the merge takes, it may sort each object's
  instead, and read all it holds: ten environments of 300 entries each
  read 3,000 where the merge needed 2,000. It reads more only as far as its
  statistics are wrong, which we could not make happen: a table grown
  seventeenfold since its last `ANALYZE` still streamed, since Postgres
  scales its counts by the table's size. A sort forced on objects of 6,000
  entries reads all 60,000 (`packages/server/test/audit.test.ts` holds
  both plans, and the same page from each). A
  `DISTINCT` over the merge reads as little only while Postgres plans it as
  a merge: past a few dozen objects it hashes every object's newest
  instead. On a log of a million entries, an Auditor of 10 projects and
  their 200 environments read 2,210 index rows for a stretch of 1,000 in
  16 ms, where the `DISTINCT` read 210,000 in 175 ms; at a thousand
  objects, 66 ms against 455. A page about more than a thousand objects
  (`MOST_OBJECTS`; Postgres runs out of stack nesting ten thousand) reads
  `log` itself, and the body decides as below: an Auditor of most of a
  large instance is shown what the index would have found, a page at a
  time that shows fewer. A page shows up to `limit` entries, counts the
  detail ones it leaves out, and ends where it stopped reading: `next`, the
  `before` of the page below, or null once the stretch reached the log's
  first entry. So a page may show fewer than `limit` and go on, and pages
  tile the log. On a log of 400,000 entries a page of 200 read 239 index
  rows across nine objects in 1.5 ms, and 201 rows of `log` in 0.2 ms.
- **The body decides.** The index only finds entries: whether one is the
  path's, the actor's, or the caller's to see is read from its body, which
  the proof covers. An index row a writer added shows nothing new.
- **Who sees what**, as 0.14.7: whoever reads the whole log
  (`readsWholeLog`) reads every entry. Anyone else reads an entry when they
  hold `audit.read` at every place it names: each environment at the
  environment, a project named alone at the project. An entry that names no
  place, a sign-in or a member's removal, is the instance's. A path the
  caller may not read the log of is refused as one that is not there; one
  they may read shows its entries by the same rule, so a folder's renaming
  that names market and billing shows under `market` to whoever reads
  both, and to no one who reads market alone.
- **Holes**, on a page of the whole log with no path or actor, under a head.
  An entry says how many of each tree come before it: its own tree's, its
  leaf; the other's, its seq less its leaf. Going down from the head, or
  from the entry the page above ended at, which the page reads with its
  stretch, each entry must follow the last with nothing of either tree
  between, and the last page must reach the kept tree's first entry and the
  aging tree's first that is not pruned. What is missing is a hole, counted
  by tree. A filtered page has none to say: nothing holds `log_index` to the
  log. Two limits. A prune between the vault's read of the head and the
  page's read shows uses as missing until a reload. And a seq is under no
  tree: a writer who deletes an entry and numbers those after it again can
  move the hole off the page, which the signer's re-check finds by leaf.

## What grows with what

| Operation | Cost |
|---|---|
| Use | 0 to 2 database calls |
| Change | 2 calls |
| Page | 1 to 3 indexed queries of 50 rows |
| Signing | The entries written since the last run |
| Minute check | One pass over the starred rows |
| Pruning | At most 40 blocks of 256 uses a minute, read by index in two statements, and one in the minutes with nothing to prune |
| Re-check | 3 blocks of 256 entries a minute, read by index in one statement |
| Deletions | 100 rows of each kind that lapsed a minute: one change, and one statement |
| Key rotation | Once over the starred rows and the versions, and only when asked |
| `coffre verify --full` | The whole log that is kept, and only when asked |

Nothing but the full verify grows with the log. On a log of 2 million
entries with two index rows each, the re-check's read takes
1.1 ms and 108 buffers, and pruning 40 blocks, their 20,480 index rows and
40 tiles, 300 ms, each by its primary key or `log_leaf_key`, with no index
added. A third of that is hashing each entry again to its tile; a move
with nothing to prune adds 0.2 ms. The index rows go by their exact keys, an object and an entry's
number, one probe each. So an object a million changes mention between two
pruned uses costs nothing more: by object within the uses' numbers, the
same delete read those million rows, 2.46 million in all, in 1.48 s.

The deletions' read takes 49 ms on 200,000 credentials, 50,000 device
logins and 10,000 enrollment codes: the credentials and the logins by
their expiry's index, the codes by one pass over the credentials that
finds those no credential names. Their write costs what the foreign keys
check, with no index added: each code deleted is one pass over the
credentials (`credentials.enrollment_id`), and each credential one over the
MCP approvals, to delete its own. A hundred of each took 2.7 s there, under
the write's 5 s; a code lapses once a deploy, so a minute deletes one or
none, and the upkeep keeps both tables at what is live and a day. Past 400,000
credentials, a hundred codes at once would need an index on
`credentials.enrollment_id`. The rows that carry no MAC go in 3 ms.

The registrations no connection names are read through
`credentials_client_idx`, the client's id each MCP credential keeps: the
MCP rows only, never the other credentials. On 200,000 credentials, 4,000
of them MCP, and 10,000 registrations: 0.6 ms with ten a week old, each
probed by the index, 14.8 ms with 5,000 (one pass over the index's
rows), 1.3 ms with none. The change reads it twice, as every part, the
second time under the change lock. `oauth.register`'s ceiling is an
index-only walk of the 10,000 keys, 3 ms, twice; `mcp.token` finds its
connection by its token, the refresh token it replaced or its code, each
by its own index, and its person's other connections by
`credentials_live_idx`.

The minute check grows with
the instance: principals, grants, credentials, keys. That is fine to about
100,000 rows, and past that it would be split by table.

Measured on a Postgres capped at 2 CPUs and 2 GB, with a 2-million-row log
and five services each hammering ten keys:

| One use | Database CPU | Ceiling on 2 CPUs |
|---|---|---|
| Repeat, one plain read | 67 µs | 30,000/s |
| Repeat, one lean stored function | 61 µs | 33,000/s |
| First in its minute, read then insert | 165 µs | 12,000/s |
| First in its minute, one stored function | 141 µs | 14,000/s |

So leaving the stored function costs about 10% on a repeat and 15% on a first
use, both far under what 1,000 uses a second need. The 0.15 line measured
0.74 to 0.85 ms per repeat on another bench, roughly five times more. Not
measured yet: the round trip through Workers and Hyperdrive, the vault's own
CPU for MACs and decryption, and the wake-up call.

## Rotating the vault's key

A key is replaced because someone who should not hold it may: it leaked, or a
person who had it left. So a rotation must end with the old key good for
nothing. 0.14 never gets there. Its old vault key stays configured for good,
to open what it wrapped and to verify what it signed.

What is under the vault's key, and what a rotation does with it:

| Under the key | How many | At a rotation |
|---|---|---|
| The MAC of a starred row | One per access row | Computed again |
| The wrapped data key of a version | One per version | Unwrapped and wrapped again. The value is not touched |
| The MAC of an entry in `log_pending` | A second's worth | Signed out within the second |
| `log_head`'s MAC | One | Written again at the next signing |
| An entry in `log` | The whole log | Nothing |

The last line is what lets the old key go. An entry's MAC is checked once,
when the signer takes it. From then on the tree vouches for it, and the
tree's head is in the signer's storage. A full verify computes the tree again
and needs no key.

**One rule: an instance's key is the key its policy row is under.** A vault
writes rows and entries under the key of the policy row it read, whatever key
its configuration lists first. Every use and every change reads that row
already.

For the operator it is three steps.

1. `coffre keys` makes the new key. It becomes `kek`, and the old one moves to
   `previousKeks`. Deploy.
2. Wait. The signer does the rest, and says where it is on `/readyz`, in
   Settings and in `coffre verify keys`: "rotating, 1,240 of 3,100 rows", then
   "vault-2026-10-11-hbvenp is retired, remove it".
3. Remove the old key from the configuration. Deploy.

For the signer it is three steps too.

1. **The fence.** Under the change lock, in one transaction, it signs every
   change that waits, writes the policy row's MAC under the new key, and logs
   `key.rotate`. From there on:
   - A vault with the new configuration reads the policy row under the new
     key, and writes under it.
   - A vault not yet replaced meets a row under a key it does not hold, and
     answers 503 until its deployment goes. For 2 seconds it can still answer
     uses from memory.
   - A change that read the policy row before the fence compares it at its
     write, as it compares every row it read, and starts again.
2. **The sweep.** 500 rows at a time, in primary-key order. Each batch is an
   ordinary change: it writes a row's new MAC only if the old one is still
   there, and its one entry carries both MACs of every row, so the mirror
   follows as for any change. A version's data key is unwrapped and wrapped
   again, with no entry, since versions are not in the mirror. The sweep
   keeps no cursor. Started again, it skips what has moved.
3. **Retirement.** When a pass finds nothing under the old key, no entry
   under it waits, and the fence is older than the vault's memory plus a
   minute, the signer records the old key as retired, in its own storage.

What someone can do who holds the old key and writes the database:

| When | What they can do |
|---|---|
| Before the fence | Everything. The old key is the vault's key, which is the leak itself. |
| From the fence to step 3 | A row of their own, or the old policy row put back, is served, and alerted within the minute. The signer takes no change under the old key, so the mirror never follows them. Two things are not caught: a value swapped in place and wrapped under the old key, since versions carry no MAC, and false uses added to the log, until retirement. |
| After step 3 | Nothing. A row under a key the vault does not hold is refused, and a data key that key wrapped does not open. |

So after a leak, step 3 follows step 2 at once.

**Cost.** One pass over the starred rows and the versions. 100,000 rows are
200 batches. With AWS KMS, the wrapping key rotates inside KMS and coffre
does nothing. The MAC key is then `signingKey`, which rotates as above (0.14
could never change it). Moving from a local key to KMS costs one Encrypt per
version.

**What goes with 0.14's way.**

- The old vault key configured for good.
- A rewrap command that 0.14's roadmap still owes. The sweep is that command.
- The app key's rotation. 0.14 signs every sign-in row again and signs
  everyone out. In 0.16 the app key signs no row and no entry, since the
  vault MACs them. It keeps the sign-in state, and the consent page's
  request with it; an MCP access token is a row now ("An MCP connection").
  How it rotates is designed with the server's move to the new contract.

## How long the log is kept

Changes are kept for good. Uses are pruned after 90 days by default (D229).

Uses fill the log: at most one entry per principal and key per minute. That
window is a setting, 60 seconds by default and 0 to 3,600, as 0.15's plan had
it. Equisafe wrote about 440,000 entries in its first month on 0.14, uses
and changes together, not yet counted apart. An entry with its index rows is
an estimated 0.5 to 1 KB, not measured yet. So 90 days of entries are about
1 GB there, and what ages out stays about that size.

**Sign-ins age out with the uses** (D231). A sign-in is a
change, since it writes a credential's row, and the mirror follows it like
any change. But every CI run signs in and every sidecar renews its
credential, so kept for good they would be the part of the log that never
stops growing. So the two trees are named for what happens to their entries,
not for the request's shape: one is kept, one ages out. The second holds the
uses, a session's or a workload credential's coming and going, and the
changes that were refused (D236), which changed nothing. A token a person
makes is kept. So a refused attempt older than 90 days is not in the log.

One setting, `log.keepUsesDays`: 90 by default, `null` to keep every use.
There is none for changes.

**Pruning** removes the oldest uses 256 at a time, which is one row of their
tree's lowest level: the 256 entries, their `log_index` rows and that
`log_tiles` row. The upper levels stay, and their rows hold the hash of each
pruned block. So:

- The signed head still covers every use ever logged.
- A use that is kept proves as before, from rows that are all kept.
- A full verify computes each kept block again from its entries, takes each
  pruned block's hash as stored, and must arrive at the signed root. An entry
  deleted behind the signer's back still breaks it.

What is pruned is never inferred from what is missing: a writer who deletes
a whole block and its lowest tile, changes included, leaves the tile above
holding the block's hash, and absence alone would pass. So a reader, a full
verify or a page, holds to one rule. A use is pruned if and only if its
leaf is below the `aging_from` of a head whose MAC it checked. Every use
from there to `aging_size` must be there, and every change from 0. It
reads the head and the entries in one statement, or a prune committed
between two reads shows up to 10,240 uses as missing.

The head's MAC proves the vault signed it, not that it is the latest. Say
the head holds 769 aging entries. A writer deletes entries 256 to 768 and
puts back an older head of 256, with its tiles: the reader finds that
older log whole. The signer catches it, from the head it keeps in its own
storage: the next minute's check finds the log no longer ends with the
entry it signed last. A reader catches it only by holding the head to one
it got from the signer. An older head of the same sizes differs only in a
lower `aging_from` or an earlier date, so it can only raise a false alarm;
the same check finds it too, since it holds `log_head` to the head signed
last, column for column.

Pages hold only what they show to the head: they do not look for what is
absent. Nothing holds `log_index` to the log, so a deleted index row hides
an entry from a page filtered by object with no finding there; the entry
itself stays in the log, where the re-check holds it to what was signed.

A pruned use leaves 1/8 of a byte behind. `@coffre/core/log` holds the tree
and this property: with every lowest-level row below a cut deleted, the head
is unchanged, and every kept entry and every later size still prove.

The signer prunes in its minute job, at most 40 blocks a run, about 10,000
uses, so a run costs the same whatever the backlog. It records the first use
it kept in its own storage, and in `log_head` for the pages
(`packages/vault/src/upkeep.ts`).

- **The age is the one signed.** A block is read with its tiles, held to the
  signer's own root, and each entry to its hash in the block's tile: only
  then is each entry's date, in its body, read. A date edited in the log
  breaks the entry's hash, so the block is kept, and the re-check names it.
- **Every entry past the keep.** One use younger than 90 days keeps its
  block, and the blocks after it.
- **Never the last block.** The block of the tree's last entry stays, since
  the minute's check reads where each tree ends.
- **Nothing it cannot read as signed.** A block with an entry deleted or
  edited, or whose tiles do not prove, is kept, whatever its age: the
  re-check says it, once a walk, and pruning waits there until a backup is
  restored.
- **Pruning is a move.** The signer writes the first use it now keeps, with
  the head it signs, to its storage as the move it owes; then one statement
  deletes the blocks' entries, their index rows and tiles, and writes the
  head. A signer that dies between the two is finished by the next, as any
  move is, and the statement run twice deletes nothing more. So no order
  of writes leaves rows gone that the signer does not know it pruned.
- **Only what it read.** The move names each block's tile as the signer
  proved it, by its SHA-256, and the statement deletes only the entries
  that hash to their place in a tile that still matches. A body a writer
  swaps between the read and the delete, while a lock holds the delete off,
  stays where it is, and is never parsed for its index rows: a row that
  does not parse as JSON would otherwise fail the move, and every run after
  it, for good. What stays is below the first use kept, which readers take
  as pruned, as they would a row a writer adds there.

Example: the signer last kept from use 2,560. Blocks 10 to 12 are past the
keep; block 13 holds a use of 89 days. It writes "keep from 3,328" and the
move, then deletes uses 2,560 to 3,327, their index rows and tiles 10 to
12. It dies before the delete: the next run deletes them first, then signs.

A writer who moves an entry into a block being pruned, by its tree and leaf
columns, has it deleted with the block, as if they had deleted it
themselves: its own place is then empty, and the re-check names it. The
signer never moves the first use kept on for it.

**What outlives a pruned use.** `exposures`, which says who has seen which
key and when last: the offboarding report and the bulk cap read it, never the
log. Example: Bo read `STRIPE_KEY` every day for a year and leaves. The log
has his last 90 days of reads, and `exposures` says he saw `STRIPE_KEY`, last
on the day he left. That is what decides which keys to rotate.

**What is lost.** When and how often a key was read, and who signed in when,
past 90 days.
`coffre log export --uses --before <date>` writes uses to a file, one JSON
line each, for whoever must keep them longer.

**What is never lost.** Who granted Bo `prod` two years ago, who changed a
value, who made a token: every change, with its proof.

**The same job bounds three more things** (`expire` in
`packages/vault/src/upkeep.ts`), the oldest first, 100 of each a minute: a
CI run leaves a session and a spent ID token, so that is 144,000 runs a day.

- A credential is deleted a day after it expires, and so are device logins
  and enrollment codes, the one-shot rows that carry a MAC: in one change a
  minute, an ordinary one, under the tree that ages out, so the mirror
  follows. Without this, the minute's check would grow with every CI run
  that ever signed in. The day is a margin: a client that asks just after
  its row lapsed is told it expired, not that it is unknown. An enrollment
  code goes once no credential names it: a session made from it holds it
  until that session goes. A registered OAuth client goes a week after it
  was made once no MCP credential names it: `credentials_client_idx`
  indexes each one's `client::jsonb ->> 'id'`, and the sweep asks it with a `NOT
  EXISTS` per candidate. The index casts `client::jsonb`, so a row written
  behind the vault whose `client` is not JSON is refused at its insert.
- Spent ID tokens two hours after they were spent, and MCP approvals a day
  after their expiry, carry no MAC and are deleted by one statement with no
  entry: nothing reads them after. An approval also goes with its
  connection.
- A row whose MAC is not the vault's is not deleted: no decision sees it.
  So a writer who moves back the expiry of a hundred rows, under their old
  MACs, holds the head of the batch, and the deletions stop. The rows they
  edit stop working, and the minute's check, which compares MACs, does not
  name them.
- `audit_archive`, 0.14's history, is kept until the operator drops it.

## What the models hold

Three models in [Quint](https://quint.sh), checked exhaustively by TLC and
sampled by Quint's simulator (D224). They are in `formal/quint/`.

`change.qnt` covers a change, "always an Owner", and revocation against the
short memory. A change of a role reads the caller, the target and every
Owner, and its write compares all of them. A row's MAC is a function of its
content: a row changed and changed back compares equal, and the change that
read it lands, since its decision holds for that content. TLC explores
24,627,382 distinct states with no violation. Two variants must fail and do:
a write that compares only the row it writes, and a memory dated when the
use answers.

`log.qnt` covers commits, the wake-up, the signer and its mirror, and a
writer who can put back any MAC the vault once made, delete or replay any
entry, and cut the log. It has one tree. The two trees follow the same steps
and are signed together, so it stands for each. Its
writer deletes any entry of the log, and the check holds only what the
minute's run does: that the log ends with the entry signed last. An entry
deleted further back is the re-check's (D222), not modelled. It dates
an entry at its commit; the code dates it as its write is sent and bounds
the write at 7 seconds, 22 on Workers with Hyperdrive's wait for a
connection, which the window, a minute, outlasts. Nor does it
model the batch of 256: tests hold that. A change reads one row beside the
one it writes, and the model holds the signer to a row it found, not to one
that was missing. With two rows, each is only there or not, so the model
never has a change read a row that holds an older MAC of its own, the
example above: a signer test on Postgres holds that case. Nor does it have
a change that writes nothing, whose read rows the code checks all the same.
Pruning is not modelled, nor the re-check: they run in the minute's run,
after its move, and one run is in progress at a time, in the one object.
Pruning touches only blocks that are complete, signed, moved, before the
tree's last one and before every leaf a move writes; and it is a move
itself, written to the signer's storage before its statement, so a restart
anywhere is the restart of a move, which the model holds. Two objects at
once would sign the same entries twice and break the log without pruning:
the signer relies on Cloudflare running one object per name, as its steps
already do. Tests hold pruning to every order of its writes
(`packages/vault/test/upkeep.test.ts`). The deletions are changes, which
`change.qnt` covers, and plain deletes of rows no decision reads.
TLC explores two configurations with no violation, both
small: one row, three MACs, three entries and two moves by the writer
(74,336,046 states), and two rows, each there or not (260,671,515 states). Ten
variants, each with one protection off, must fail and do. They are the
signer's requirements.

| Protection | Without it |
|---|---|
| The vault tells the signer of each entry | A deleted entry is never missed |
| A change must replace the MAC the mirror holds | False history sums to a true state |
| A change must have decided on the MAC the mirror holds | A change decided on a row put back for it is signed, and nothing ever differs |
| The log must end with the entry signed last | A log cut back passes |
| An entry already signed is refused | A replayed entry is signed twice |
| An entry dated before the signer's window is refused | An old entry is replayed later |
| A wake-up for a signed entry is dropped | A false alert |
| What is expected is noted before the read | A false alert |
| Rows and waiting entries come from one read | A false alert |
| What was signed is kept until it is moved | A false alert |

The false alerts matter because a finding only alerts. An alert nobody trusts
is worth nothing.

`rotate.qnt` covers a key rotation: a vault not yet replaced and a new one,
both changing and using through the fence, the sweep and the retirement, and
a writer who holds the old key. TLC explores 12,294,943 distinct states with
no violation: a rotation with nobody writing behind the vault raises no
alert, and the mirror never takes a row the writer made. Five variants must
fail and do.

| Protection | Without it |
|---|---|
| A change compares the policy row it read | A change read before the fence lands under the old key after it, and is refused: a false alert |
| A vault writes under the policy row's key | A new vault writes under the new key before the fence, and is refused: a false alert |
| The fence signs the changes that wait | One still waiting is refused after it: a false alert |
| Retirement waits out the vault's memory | A use answered from memory logs under a retired key: a false alert |
| After the fence, the signer takes no change under the old key | The mirror follows a forged row, and nothing ever alerts |

Trace replay holds the code to the model. Quint writes its simulated runs as
JSON, and a harness replays each step against the vault and compares after
each one. `packages/vault/test/replay-change.ts` does it for a change and a
use, on Postgres: 1,000 runs of `change.qnt`, 41,000 steps, in under three
minutes.

- **Changes:** the rows must be the model's after every step. 880 writes
  lost their race, as the model said they would. It caught both faults
  planted in the write (one that compares only the row it writes, one that
  always lands).
- **Uses:** the vault's memory must hold a read exactly when the model's
  does, and each answer must be the model's. 2,508 uses read the database
  and 12,094 were answered from memory. It caught the three faults planted
  in the use: a memory dated at the answer, a memory that never goes stale,
  and a removal's generation not compared.

`packages/vault/test/replay-log.ts` does the same for the signer, with the
vault's change and a database writer beside it: 1,000 runs of `log.qnt`,
31,000 steps, 2,000 moves by the writer. After each step the log and the signer's
mirror must be the model's; where the model alerts the signer must have
found something; and the signer must find nothing unless the writer acted
or a wake-up came late. Its first run found a fault no test and no review
had: when the signer refused one entry, its statement removed every entry
that waited, because a name in it was read as a column of the table around
it.

The model must write each refusal as a step of its own, or nothing holds the
code to refusing.

## Upgrade from 0.14

In place, with coffre offline, and a backup as the way back (D230, as D197
had it for the 0.15 plan). No second set of tables and no switch to build:
the upgrade runs once per deployment, and Equisafe's can be down for
minutes.

Three facts about 0.14 shape it.

1. **`coffre migrate` holds no vault key.** It runs in the deployment's
   pipeline with the database owner's URL. It cannot MAC a row or open a
   value, so the vault must, at its first start.
2. **0.14 keeps some state only in its audit log:** the roles and the
   protected environments (the newest `policy.change`), revoked bindings
   (`token.unbind`), workload freezes, superseded enrollments, ended
   references.
3. **0.14's code cannot run on 0.16's tables.** So this one migration breaks
   the rule that a migration only adds, as 0.4.0's did, and `pnpm
   test:compat` is off for 0.16.0.

So the work is split in two. **The migration** does everything SQL can do
without a key. **The adoption**, in the vault at its first call, does what
needs the vault's key or the access rules' code.

### The steps

1. **Verify.** `coffre verify log`, on 0.14.7.
2. **Take coffre offline, then back up.** As the database's administrator,
   set the two runtime logins `NOLOGIN` and end their sessions. Nothing coffre
   runs can reach the database from here on. Then `pg_dump`. The backup holds
   every write 0.14 ever made.
3. **Migrate and deploy** 0.16.0, through the pipeline as for any release:
   `coffre migrate`, the vault, the app.
   - The migration is one transaction. It reshapes 0.14's tables into
     0.16's, creates the new ones, renames `audit_log` to `audit_archive` and
     drops its indexes. It turns what 0.14 kept in its log into rows, while
     the log's indexes are still there to find it: live references, revoked
     bindings, freezes, superseded codes, the run a credential was issued
     for, and who has seen which key. It leaves every starred row with an
     empty MAC, which verifies under no key.
   - It refuses a database that holds an instance while a login of the
     app's or the vault's group can still log in or has a session open. So
     a pipeline run without step 2 changes nothing, fails, and the deploy
     does not happen. A login that administers roles does not count: coffre
     never runs as one.
   - It refuses one where 0.14's vault reported a member's row changed around
     it and that report still stands, and names them: 0.16 would take the row
     as it is. Each is removed on 0.14.7 first, and added again if they
     should stay. The member may well be healthy, their genuine row put
     back: 0.14 keeps the report standing all the same.
4. **Bring it back.** Set the two logins `LOGIN`. At the vault's first call
   the signer's object adopts the database, in one transaction (`adopt` in
   `packages/vault/src/adopt.ts`). What says the database is new is the
   signer, not the database: `adopt` takes the signer, and refuses if it
   has signed an entry or been told of one, even one it has since
   reported missing (it keeps that it was told). So a writer who empties
   the log, deletes the roles and the policy and plants an Owner on an
   adopted instance has nothing adopted again: the vault stays on 503, and
   the signer's next check finds the log cut or the entry missing. The one
   gap is a wake that fails after the adoption commits: until the signer
   reads the entry, deleting it makes the database look new to it. The database says only whether
   an adoption is under way: under the change lock, `adopt` does nothing if
   an entry is signed or waiting, or a head counts one. So two calls at
   once adopt once: the second waits for the lock, then finds the first's
   entry. A head alone would not do: between the commit and the first
   signature there is none. And a head that counts no entry is the
   signer's refusal of something that waited before any adoption; the
   adoption follows it.
   - It writes the roles, the policy row and which environments are
     protected, from the newest `policy.change` and `settings.change` the
     vault logged in `audit_archive`, each checked against the vault's MAC,
     and whether the instance is frozen. The roles as they ship are in the
     code, not in SQL, which is why this part is the vault's. It makes the
     Owners the configuration names Owners of the whole instance, brought
     back if removed, as 0.14 did at each start.
   - It wraps each version's data key again, so that the wrap names its
     version number and whether it is plain, as `envelope_version` 2. 0.14
     knew a plain value only by its `plaintext` column, which nothing
     protects, so the adoption opens each one and takes it as plain only if
     the value is that column's, and stops otherwise: a writer who marks
     `STRIPE_KEY` plain before the upgrade would otherwise have it handed,
     from then on, to whoever lists its environment. It decrypts every
     value, so a ciphertext moved under another version's key stops it too.
     The data key and the ciphertext are kept. It skips a version 0.14
     erased, whose ciphertext and data key are empty: a deleted place's, a
     deleted personal config's. Such a version can still be its secret's
     current one, in a deleted place: it opens as nothing, and a use of it is
     refused.
   - It writes on each secret's row the nature its current value opened
     as, whatever the migration or a writer since left there, in the
     statement that MACs the row, from the row as it read it: a row a
     writer changes while it runs keeps a MAC over what it no longer
     holds, and is no row. One whose current value it did not open, an
     erased one, is taken as sensitive, the nature that hands a value to
     the fewest; one with no current value, none. A value opened as
     plain is one whose `plaintext` column is its value, which nothing
     protects ([What it trusts](#what-it-trusts)). The recipe and pair the
     migration moved onto the row are taken as they stand: 0.14 bound them
     to nothing ([What it trusts](#what-it-trusts)).
   - It MACs every starred row.
   - It writes the log's first entry, `vault.adopt`, which records 0.14's
     last hash and entry number, and how many rows of each table it
     adopted. It names no row: 100,000 would not fit an entry.
   - It commits, and wakes the signer. The signer signs that entry with the
     rows read in the same statement, and its mirror starts as those rows. A
     signer that dies in between finds the entry unsigned and does only this
     last part.

   Until that first entry is signed, requests answer 503 `adopting`
   (`adopted`, a head that counts an entry). Not only until the commit: a change served in
   between would be in the rows the mirror starts from, and the signer would
   hold its entry's `was` to a mirror that already has its `now`, a false
   `rewritten`. If anything fails (a policy entry whose MAC is wrong, a value
   that does not open), the transaction is undone, the 503 stays, and
   `/readyz` says what failed.

   Measured on the local key, with 10,000 versions and 100,000 starred rows:
   the adoption takes 5 seconds, 2.2 of them the vault's CPU, and the
   signer's first run 0.4. A key service is called twice per version, to
   unwrap and to wrap, all in the object's one call: 2N subrequests for N
   versions, 20,000 there. Workers allow 10,000 per invocation by default
   on the Paid plan, and an adoption past it fails whole each time it is
   tried. So a vault on a key service with more than 4,900 versions sets
   its Worker's `limits.subrequests` to 2N and a hundred more before the
   upgrade. A local key makes none.
5. **Watch** `/readyz`, the first signed head and the first minute's check.

A fresh install runs the same code: the migration creates the tables, and
the signer adopts an empty database and the Owners its configuration names.

The adoption reads no 0.14 table but `audit_archive`: the migration leaves no
other.

### What becomes what

| `secrets`, `secret_references` | `secrets`. A reference still live becomes `source_id`, and its `created_by` and `created_at` its `source_set_by`, as the member it names now, and `source_set_at`. `current_version` becomes `versions`, how many it has had, and `current_version_id` becomes `current_version`, a number: the newest, or none. The current version's nature, recipe and pair are the secret's, which the adoption writes the nature of again |
| `secret_versions` | Kept, but for the recipe and pair, which go to the secret's row: an older version's go. Each gains `first_written_at`, the first `created_at` among its key's versions with its IV and tag: 0.14's restore copied its source's ciphertext, so that is how 0.14 told a restored value. | `vault_members` | `principals`. A frozen service account says so in its row |

One fact 0.14's pages read from the log moves into the credential's row: the
CI run that issued it.

Who a row names becomes the member 0.16 names. 0.14.7's app wrote the
caller's bare id in the columns it owned (`secret_versions.created_by`,
`secret_references.created_by`, which becomes `secrets.source_set_by`,
`dismissed_keys.dismissed_by` and `restored_by`, and `created_by` and
`revoked_by` of `identities`, `service_bindings` and `credentials`): Ada
as `ada@acme.example`, the service account `ci-deploy` as `ci-deploy`. Its
vault, and 0.16 everywhere, write the principal, `user:ada@acme.example` or
`token:ci-deploy`, and the member report asks with it. The migration
resolves each by membership, removed members included: the text as it is
if it already names a member (an older release's principal, or the revoker
a binding's tombstone gives), then a person, then a service account, else
the text as it is. A service account named exactly as a member's email, or
as a member's principal (`user:ada@acme.example`), is taken for the member.
A column's literals
are never resolved: `binding:<id>`, which says a credential stands on the
binding that issued it, `coffre`, the key rotation's revocations, and
`system:…`. So a service account named `binding:<id>` on 0.14 does not turn
that binding's credential into one that outlives it.

The migration is `0022_smaller_vault.sql`. `pnpm test:upgrade` holds it to
this table: it upgrades a 0.14 database with a row of every kind, on a
cluster of its own, and requires that database, one migrated from nothing and
one made from `schema.ts` alone to have the same schema, line for line.

### What does not change for anyone

- **Tokens and sessions.** A token is found by its SHA-256, as before, so
  every live session, service token, personal token and workload credential
  keeps working. Nobody signs in again, and no sidecar enrolls again.
- **Clients.** 0.16.0's HTTP API takes 0.14.7's CLI, Action, sidecar image
  and client package. This is a requirement on the build, and conformance
  must hold it: the clients are upgraded after the server, not with it.
- **Keys.** The vault key and the app key stay as configured.

### Behind Cloudflare Access

0.14.7 took Access's assertion as the caller, for every request; 0.16 takes
it as a sign-in (see "Behind Cloudflare Access", in "The server"). For a
deployment behind Access:

- **A browser signs in on its first request** and holds a session of
  coffre's: it shows in the person's sessions, its sign-in is in the log,
  and removing them ends it, as with GitHub. Nobody does anything for it.
- **An account binds by Access's subject**, the first time by its email.
  0.14 Access deployments have no identity rows, so everyone binds once.
  Someone whose Access `sub` changes later (their IdP user made again) is
  refused until an admin removes them and adds them again; 0.14.7 matched
  the email.
- **Signing out ends coffre's session** too, then Access's.
- **The CLI and machines are not served behind Access yet**: `coffre login`
  through `cloudflared` and Access service tokens get 401, saying so, until
  they sign in with coffre's own credentials. A deployment whose CLI or
  machines reach coffre through Access waits for that before it upgrades.

### Going back

- **Before the migration:** set the two logins `LOGIN`. Nothing changed.
- **After it:** restore the backup, deploy 0.14.7, set the two logins
  `LOGIN`. A login's `NOLOGIN` is the cluster's, not the database's, so the
  restore leaves it as it is. What was written on 0.16 since is lost.
- **To upgrade again after going back:** empty the signer's storage first.
  The adoption refuses a signer that has signed an entry or been told of
  one.

### What it trusts

The adoption takes the database as it finds it. It checks that every value
opens, and the MAC of the two log entries it reads. That MAC check is a
tripwire for a vault given another signing key than 0.14's, not a defence:
it takes any key the vault is given, at any entry, where 0.14 took a
replaced key only before its rotation, and a writer who cannot forge the
newest `policy.change` deletes it instead.

The rows 0.14's app signed are taken as they stand at the upgrade, whenever
they were written: sessions and tokens, linked accounts, trust bindings,
device and enrollment codes, OAuth clients. 0.14 checked one only when it
was used, the vault does not hold the app key, and the migration drops their
MACs. So a session row a database writer planted on 0.14 for a real Owner,
and never used, becomes a genuine Owner session (D237). For the same reason
the adoption does not check 0.14's seal on a member's row: a writer refused
a forged member would plant a session for a real Owner instead.

For the rest, the runbook verifies the log on 0.14.7 first, and the stretch
from that verification to the adoption, minutes, is the one where a
database writer's edit to a member, a grant or a reference would be adopted
as genuine. The same holds for values: a
version's row swapped with another of its secret before the adoption is
wrapped again as it stands, and is genuine from then on. And for a
recipe, which 0.14 bound to nothing: one a writer set to `bytes:1:hex` on
0.14, or in that stretch, is sealed on the secret's row, and Regenerate
makes the next value by it. A value's nature is taken so only for plain:
the adoption writes it from the value it opens, and 0.14's wrap names a
sensitive one, but a plain one is known only by its `plaintext` column,
so a writer who knows a value can set that column and have it adopted
plain. A wrong guess stops the adoption. And for the log:
the adoption reads the newest `policy.change` and `settings.change`, so one
deleted in that stretch has the one before it adopted, and the instance's
freeze is the app's newest `workload.freeze` or lift naming it, under the
app's MAC, which the vault cannot check, as the migration reads every other
freeze. The verification is what holds the log whole up to then. The
stretch ends at the first entry's signature, not the commit: what a writer
changes in the second between is in the rows the mirror starts from.

### Downtime

From step 2 to the adoption's first entry signed: the backup, the
pipeline's run (the migration and two deploys), then the adoption. For Equisafe I expect a few
minutes, most of them the pipeline's, and the rehearsal measures it.

### Refused

- A database below 0.14.5's last migration: upgrade to 0.14.7 first.
- A deployment that never ran 0.14: there is no path from an earlier
  release.

### Rehearsal, before any release

On a copy of Equisafe's database, in a throwaway deployment. The copy only
adopts with Equisafe's real vault key, so Erwin puts that key into the
throwaway vault himself. It runs the steps above as written, measures the
migration, the adoption and the downtime, compares the row counts with the
table above, runs 0.14.7's sidecar against the result, and goes back once
from the backup. If the vault's key is a key service's, the rehearsal uses
that service, with the vault Worker's
`limits.subrequests` set as in step 4: the local key makes no call.

### At Equisafe

- `vault/wrangler.jsonc` gains the signer's Durable Object binding and its
  migration. `coffre update` writes both.
- `app/wrangler.jsonc` keeps `CoffreAnchor` until the release after, so that
  going back finds its storage.
- The deploy workflow is unchanged: it migrates, then deploys the vault,
  then the app.
- The backend's sidecar stays on its 0.14 image until the Workers have run
  0.16.0 for a while.

## Build order

0.16 starts from `release/0.14`, the code Equisafe runs. The 0.15 line stays
on `main` as the source to port from, and nothing merges from it.

The base work merges into `0.16`, which stays green. The core is built on
`0.16-core`, which is not: its first commit replaces the schema, and 0.14's
vault and server do not build on it. Each piece of the core is held to its
own tests there, and `0.16-core` merges into `0.16` once the server has moved
and everything is green again.

1. **A smaller base.** Postgres as the only database, which deletes SQLite's
   schema, migrations and test engine. One Postgres driver, postgres.js. Local
   checks and a release gate in place of CI on every pull request. Each of
   these halves what the next steps must build or test.
2. **The core.** The schema and its migration, the vault's two shapes, the
   signer, the models in `pnpm formal`, trace replay in the tests, and the
   server's calls moved to the new contract. 0.14's audit chain and its TLA+
   models go. With the server and the CLI: the check that a configured vault
   key is the one that wrapped the values, and `coffre restore`'s check of a
   backup, both of which read 0.14's envelope format today.
3. **The features 0.15's line added**, each written once against the new
   contract: passkeys at sign-in, proxy routes saved per environment with
   their Proxy tab, the Terraform provider, the pages' rework. Then the small
   fixes, each checked against 0.14's code first, since some fixed bugs 0.14
   never had.

The source for step 3 is main's changelog since 0.14.5, 136 entries. Those
that begin "0.15's" describe the design this replaces and are not ported.

## Still to design

- The signer on Node. 0.16.0 is tested on Workers only.
- The minute check past 100,000 rows.
- The app key's rotation, now that it signs no row.
- The CLI and machines behind Cloudflare Access. A browser signs in there
  (see "Behind Cloudflare Access"). The CLI and machines will use coffre's
  own credentials, with Access at the edge alone: `coffre login` runs the
  device login, approved in a browser Access signed in, and sends both
  Access's token and coffre's bearer; a machine uses a service token or a
  binding of coffre's, with an Access service token at the edge.
- A bound of the vault's own on a use's read: an outage in which the
  database takes connections and answers nothing is not one until the
  connection's own timeout says so (D238).
- Losing the signer's storage: how an Owner tells the signer to take the
  database as it is, and what that is logged as. 0.14 has the same question
  for its anchor.
